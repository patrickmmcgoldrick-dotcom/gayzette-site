Google's Gemini 4 Argon is now open to everyone, at half price for now

Google's newest model left limited access on October 13, with a launch price that doubles on December 1.

The Gayzette · Wonder ◆◆◆◇◇

Google moved its newest model, Gemini 4 Argon, into general availability on October 13, according to its own changelog (the public list of what changed). Google calls it its most capable model for complex coding, agent work (software that takes several steps on its own), math and science. The models page lists it as stable, with a 1M-token context window, meaning it can hold about a million small chunks of text in view at once.

The price is the story. Through November 30, Standard use costs $2.00 per million input tokens and $10.00 per million output tokens, including its thinking steps. From December 1 that becomes $4 and $20. Batch and Flex (slower, cheaper queues) are half of Standard until November 30. There is no free tier, and it is not part of the Google AI Pro plan. These numbers come from Google's own pages, and we have not seen outside testing.

On your desk: if any of your n8n (the automation tool) workflows use a Gemini node, run one real job through Argon before November 30, and put the December 1 doubling on the calendar.

AI Builder

Claude Code now tidies long idle chats before the cache runs out

Claude Code version 2.1.299, released October 14, adds idle compaction in the terminal. If a conversation is over 200K tokens and sits idle for about 54 minutes, Claude Code now summarizes it before the one-hour prompt cache (a short-term memory that makes resumed chats cheaper) expires. You can tune it with CLAUDE_CODE_IDLE_COMPACT_MIN_TOKENS, or switch it off with "idleCompaction": false.

Some changes can break things. MCP allow rules now match only the named server, so use mcp__<server>__* for a whole server. Skills and commands with hidden or blank-like characters, or < > in their names, will not load until renamed. On your desk: after updating, open your custom skills and MCP permission rules and check that each one still loads.

Spirit

Ketamine and psychedelics leave a similar mark on immune signals

A study led by Gregory H. Jones of the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) and MD Anderson, reported by PsyPost on October 13, compared ketamine, LSD and psilocybin. The team treated brain cells grown from stem cells with the drugs. Of 103 proteins that ketamine changed in spinal fluid, 86% were also changed by at least one psychedelic. They clustered around immune signaling and around insulin and neural rewiring.

In a trial of 39 people with treatment-resistant depression and 25 healthy volunteers, immune markers before a single ketamine dose tracked who responded, and reversed 4 hours later. Caveat: the authors call the work "primarily exploratory," and the cells came from female donors only. We read PsyPost's account, not the paper itself.

On your desk: for your meditation notes, this is one early hint that altered states and the immune system may talk to each other. Treat it as a question, not an answer.

More in Spirit

FDA sketches what a careful first ibogaine trial could look like

Read the brief

This one is older than our usual window: the event was Monday, October 5, and the Psychedelic Alpha bulletin reporting it is dated October 8. The FDA put out a request for information (a public call for feedback) describing a hypothetical early trial of ibogaine, a plant-derived psychedelic. Doses would rise in steps, capped at 10 mg/kg, one participant at a time.

Participants would be dosed as inpatients with continuous heart monitoring for up to 36 hours, limited to certain fast or intermediate CYP2D6 metabolizers (people whose bodies process the drug at set speeds), with five stopping rules and 12 months of thinking-and-memory tests. It is part of an HHS effort with NIDA and ARPA-H. This is secondary reporting; we did not open the FDA page. On your desk: file it as a sign that regulators are planning how to test these compounds safely.
