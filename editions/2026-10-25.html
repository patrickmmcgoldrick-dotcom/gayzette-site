An AI-made proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis meets its human readers

OpenAI announced the proof; mathematicians are now working through a 49-page version, and the famous original problem stays unsolved.

By The Gayzette, with Claude · 2 min read · Wonder ◆◆◆◆◇

Some math problems are famous because they are hard. The Riemann hypothesis has been hard since 1859 and is still unsolved. It concerns the zeta function, a formula mathematicians use to study the prime numbers, and in particular its "nontrivial zeros", the special inputs where that formula gives back zero.

Quanta Magazine's Konstantin Kakaes reports that OpenAI announced a proof of something smaller: the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, a weaker cousin of the big one. Think of the original as asking exactly where every animal in a field is standing, and the quasi version as asking you to prove the fence holds them all inside a band of the field. Less ambitious, still a real result if it stands.

There are two versions. The first ran 199 pages and was checked in Lean, a proof assistant (software that confirms every logical step follows from the last), and it put the zeros between 1/8 and 7/8. A second, 49-page version puts them between 1/12 and 11/12, a wider band and so a weaker result. It has not been formally verified, but it is the one mathematicians are mostly reading, because it is shorter.

The experts are taking it seriously. Maksym Radziwiłł of NYU calls the implications "staggering", though he also calls both papers "horribly written". Alexander Dunn of Georgia Tech calls the proof "a surprising synthesis of well-known ideas", and the argument relies on metaplectic forms, a specialized family of functions that he has studied for about five years. So the machine did not work from nothing; it built on a human's tools.

The caveat: the proof is still under expert review, and the shorter version people are reading has not been machine-checked. "AI proved it" becomes a settled sentence only after the mathematicians finish reading.

Indonesia cut extreme poverty from 86% to under 4% in four decades

About 140 million people below the World Bank's $3-a-day line in 1984; about 10 million in 2025.

"Extreme poverty" sounds vague until someone draws the line. Here the line is the World Bank's $3 a day: if what you live on falls below it, you count as extremely poor. One fixed bar lets you compare a country with itself across decades.

In a new Our World in Data insight, Hannah Ritchie and Pablo Arriagada chart Indonesia against that bar. In 1984, 86% of Indonesians lived below it. In 2025 the figure was under 4%; the chart shows about 3.7%. In people, that is roughly 140 million down to about 10 million. The data come from the World Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform (2026).

Picture a room of 100 Indonesians in 1984: 86 of them below the line. Walk into the same room in 2025 and fewer than four are.

The piece frames Indonesia as the quieter story next to China, whose fall in extreme poverty is the one everyone already knows. That is part of what makes it useful: a huge change that never became a headline, sitting in a public chart anyone can open and check.

The honest caveat comes from Positive News, which picked up the chart: critics credit cash handouts more than a broad rise in living standards. So the number tells you how many people crossed the line, not how far past it they got, or what is keeping them there.

Before a new drug can be sold in the US, its maker files a new drug application, or NDA: the formal case to the FDA that the drug works and is safe enough. A rolling review lets the company hand that case in section by section, like turning in a thesis chapter by chapter, instead of all at once.

Read the brief

Compass Pathways got that rolling review in April 2026 for COMP360, its psilocybin treatment, plus a Commissioner's National Priority Voucher, a fast-track pass for the review itself. Per Psychedelics Today's weekly policy briefing and the company's own Q2 release, Compass expects to finish the submission in Q4 2026 and says the voucher could shorten review to one or two months.

The evidence it is filing: in its COMP006 trial, 39% of participants reached a clinically meaningful drop on the MADRS, a standard depression rating scale, at Week 6, and on average kept that improvement through at least Week 26. The company reported $433.3 million in cash at June 30.

Where things stand: neither psilocybin nor MDMA is approved as a medicine yet.

The caveat, said lightly: these numbers come from the company, and the Q4 date is Compass's expectation, not an FDA commitment. A finished filing starts the review; it does not decide it.

The Universe

Bennu, Ryugu and meteorites share one ancient dust fingerprint

ETH Zurich's isotope work on the OSIRIS-REx sample suggests these bodies formed where inner and outer Solar System material mixed.

Every element comes in versions called isotopes: atoms that behave the same chemically but weigh slightly different amounts. The mix of isotopes in a rock works like an accent. It tells you, roughly, where the material grew up.

Read the brief

Maria Schönbächler's team at ETH Zurich listened for that accent in a piece of the asteroid Bennu: half a gram of the roughly 120 grams NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission brought home on Sept 23, 2023. They measured isotopes of iron, titanium and chromium and compared them with samples from the asteroid Ryugu and with a well-studied group of meteorites known as CI meteorites.

All three share chemical fingerprints from one common reservoir of ancient dust. The team proposes these bodies formed near the water-ice line, the distance from the young Sun beyond which water freezes, where material from the inner and outer Solar System mixed. Jupiter, they suggest, may have screened out the larger particles. "Bennu is a hybrid," Schönbächler says.

Think of a smoothie made right on the border between two farms: some fruit from each side, blended at the fence. That is the picture of Bennu's birthplace the isotopes point to.

The caveat: this is a proposal about where these bodies formed, inferred from matching fingerprints, not a direct view of their birth, and it rests on one team's measurements of a small sample. The study is in Science Advances; we read it through ScienceDaily's posting of the ETH Zurich release.

Curiosity photographs a 10-mile wall of wind-carved ridges on Mars

The ridges may be ancient volcanic ash; the rover's team plans a close look in 2027.

On Mars the wind is the sculptor. Over very long stretches of time it can carve rock into long, parallel ridges called yardangs, a bit like a sandblaster working slowly along a wall and leaving the tougher strips standing.

Read the brief

NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory has released a panorama, stitched from six photos by Curiosity's Mastcam camera, of a band of yardangs stretching about 10 miles (16 km) across the northwest flank of Mount Sharp, a mountain roughly 3 miles tall. The photos were taken on Aug 11, 2026, which the team logs as sol 4,982 (a sol is one Martian day).

The rover has been climbing. Curiosity has recently gained 0.6 miles (1 km) in elevation, more than any other Mars rover. That climb is why it can now see this layer at all.

What is the wall made of? Scientists suspect the layer is ancient volcanic ash. Ash is a useful suspect because it records an eruption, a moment in the planet's history you can try to read.

The caveat: ash is the working guess, not a result. The team plans a close look in 2027, with no firm date yet, and until then the panorama is a beautiful question rather than an answer.

Dance's first pro league ends its season with an ESPN deal

LA's GRV won the final by 0.43 of a point; the league reports sold-out finals, prize money by standings and more than 2 billion social views.

A dance league works like a sports league: teams (here, crews) compete through a season, standings decide who earns what, and a championship crowns a winner. The International Dance League (IDL) opened its first season in May 2026.

Read the brief

Dance Magazine's Natalie Albaran recaps how it ended. On Sept 20 at the Shrine Expo Hall in Los Angeles, LA's GRV beat Vancouver's previously undefeated Brotherhood by 0.43 of a point, on a 100-point scale. That is a photo finish.

Then the twist that shows how a league differs from a contest: prize money is split by season standings, not by the final alone. Brotherhood took $200,000 and GRV $180,000. Lose the night, win the season.

The league reports an ESPN media-rights deal (the right to broadcast its events), more than 2 billion social views, and a finals that sold out in 15 minutes. Those are the league's figures.

Not everyone is cheering everything. Criticisms include community-division contests that were not streamed and questions about the scoring format. The league created a Cultural and Historical Advisory Board, including Buddha Stretch and Latasha Barnes, to keep the culture in view.

Natalia Makarova, 86, restages Paquita for ABT at Lincoln Center

She coached three principal couples through Act III with a rehearsal director she first cast at 15; the run is dedicated to Fernando Bujones.

Ballet is mostly passed on by hand. There are notations and videos, but the details, where the weight sits, how long a balance holds, travel from one dancer's body to the next in a rehearsal room. A restaging is that handoff on purpose.

Read the brief

Pointe reports that Natalia Makarova, now 86, restaged the Act III grand divertissement of Paquita (a showcase suite of dances, there to dazzle rather than to move a plot) for American Ballet Theatre's fall season at Lincoln Center, Oct 21 to 31. She coached three principal couples herself.

The room had history in it. Makarova worked with rehearsal director Nancy Raffa, whom she cast at 15 in 1980 opposite Fernando Bujones, and with artistic director Susan Jaffe. The performances are dedicated to Bujones' memory.

Think of a family recipe taught at the stove instead of copied from a card: the grandmother who learned it is in the kitchen, and so is the cousin she first taught decades ago.

The caveat is a small one: our account comes from Pointe's feature, not from ABT's own page, so check the company's listings for which couples dance which nights. The run closes Oct 31.

Books & Publishing

Hollywood's book scouts now mostly ask for backlist titles, report saysearly reports

Backlist is nearly two-thirds of film and TV requests on one rights platform, and requests for international stories rose 3.5 times.

Publishers sort books into two piles. Frontlist is what just came out. Backlist is everything that has been out a while. The usual assumption is that Hollywood chases the new pile.

Read the brief

A new report from Rightscenter, a platform where book rights are offered to film and TV buyers, says otherwise. Its "Book-to-Screen Marketplace Insights, 2019-2026" finds that backlist titles are nearly two-thirds of all requests from studios, producers and scouts. Requests for international stories rose 3.5 times on the previous year, and female protagonists appear in 48% of the titles of interest.

Co-owner Emily Parker puts it in five words: "A good book doesn't expire." Studios are not only rummaging in older piles; they are looking further afield, and that 3.5-times jump in requests for international stories says buyers are widening the map, not only digging deeper.

Think of a book on a shelf as a seed rather than a cut flower. A film adaptation can sprout from it years after publication, as long as someone is still looking at the shelf.

The caveat, said lightly: this is one rights platform's own data, reported by Publishing Perspectives, and it describes who asked on that platform, not every deal in Hollywood. Requests are interest, not purchases.

Colorado's I-25 wildlife crossings cut animal-vehicle crashes by 91%

A 200-foot-wide overpass over I-25 completed a system of fences and underpasses, and elk, pronghorn and frogs are all using it.

The idea is simple: if animals keep crossing a highway, give them their own way across, then use fences to steer them toward it. On I-25 between Castle Rock and Monument, Colorado built the full version.

Read the brief

Smithsonian Magazine reports that a $15 million overpass, 200 feet wide and spanning six lanes, opened in December 2025. It is the largest of its kind in North America, and it completed a system of 14 miles of fencing and five underpasses.

The numbers: collisions fell 91% in the final year of a 2022-2024 study period. The stretch used to see roughly one wildlife-vehicle crash a day in spring and fall; only two have been reported in the ten months since the overpass opened. Elk, pronghorn, rabbits and northern leopard frogs have all been detected using it, and travel delays on the stretch dropped about 9%.

Think of it as a pedestrian bridge whose pedestrians include a frog and an elk on the same commute. CDOT's Chuck Attardo says such crossings "pay for themselves in about 15 years."

The caveat: the 91% figure comes from the study years, before the big overpass itself opened, and ten months is a short record for the finished system. Both point the same way, but the long-run number is still being written.

AI Reads the Ancients

  • BIG STORY — [ANNOUNCED] A whole Herculaneum scroll, read end to end. PHerc. 1667 is a carbonised papyrus roll from the Villa of the Papyri at Herculaneum, buried when Vesuvius erupted in AD 79 and now held in Naples. Hand-opening attempts in the 1980s damaged it, and it was judged unreadable: a fused, charred spiral whose carbon-based ink looks almost exactly like the burnt papyrus around it, like black pencil on black paper. Only about 8 cm of its original 19-24 cm height survives. The team scanned it with high-resolution X-ray micro-CT at the ESRF in Grenoble, unwrapped it virtually in software, and used machine-learning models trained to spot the faint ink to bring out a readable surface; papyrologists helped build those models, then transcribed and reviewed the text. The result, announced June 25, 2026: roughly 1.4 metres of papyrus and around 22 columns of Greek text, an ethics treatise by an unknown author, likely Stoic and likely 2nd century BC, whose final column names Aristocreon. The readings are fragmentary, and the Stoic attribution is a lean, not a confirmed identification. The full work is a preprint; the page does not say whether it has been peer reviewed. On your desk: the Library OS shelf, as the first whole book this method has handed back. Vesuvius Challenge: the first whole scroll (PHerc. 1667)

  • NEED TO KNOW — [PUBLISHED] Imaging, no AI: why some scrolls give up text and others don't. A team including NIST researchers (PLOS ONE, Sept 16, 2026) made a stand-in scroll from modern papyrus and carbon ink, spiked the ink with a little lead to mimic lead reported in Herculaneum fragments, and charred it. X-ray tomography plus unrolling software made the writing legible, still detectable down to the lowest lead level tested, and a handheld X-ray fluorescence device found the lead on the outside, a cheap screen for which real scrolls are worth a synchrotron scan. Carbon-only ink is the harder case. This was a model scroll, not an ancient one, and no machine learning did the reading. On your desk: the Symbol & Etymology Bank, as the step before any reading: first, can you see the ink at all? The Droplet Desk

  • NEW THIS WEEK — THE SCIENCE, PLAIN: [PUBLISHED] Self-assembly means parts snapping together into a bigger structure with no one steering. University of Oxford chemists (Roi Asor, Philipp Kukura and colleagues, Nature) used mass photometry, which weighs single molecules by how much light they scatter, to watch an engineered 60-protein virus-like shell, a hollow protein ball with no genetic material, build itself step by step. Early bonds between proteins are weak and reversible, so wrong arrangements fall apart and get retried, like a jigsaw you can shake loose and try again. Certain closed shapes, starting with a ring of five larger blocks, are stable enough to act as waypoints, and after that ring each new stage needs fewer blocks, so assembly speeds up. Asor describes it happening "without a blueprint or machinery directing the process." The particle is lab-engineered, uses for antivirals and vaccines are prospective, and the paper is about viral shells, not droplets, RNA or the origin of life. ScienceDaily posted it on Oct 22, which is why it runs now.
