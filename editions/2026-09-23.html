Claude Opus 5.5 lands: near-top quality, about 40% cheaper

Anthropic's newest Opus model performs at the level of its top model on most work, and Anthropic says it costs about 40% less to run than Opus 5.

By The Gayzette, with Claude · 2 min read · Wonder ◆◆◆◇◇

Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5 yesterday, priced at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens — tokens being the chunks of text a model reads and writes, billed by the million. Anthropic says it's "40% less expensive" to run than Opus 5 on typical workloads, while performing "at the level of Claude Fable 5.1 on most work" and generating output 30% faster.

The gains show up on harder tests too. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, which grades how well a model handles real command-line tasks, Opus 5 scored 52.3%; Opus 5.5 jumped to 66.4%. GitHub's product chief, Mario Rodriguez, is quoted on Anthropic's launch page: "In VS Code, it solved more terminal tasks than Opus 5 in less than half the steps." Internal safety testing also found it 85% less likely than Opus 5 or Mythos 5.1 to try to bypass its own guardrails — the boundaries it's meant to stay inside.

Trading firm Optiver, an early tester, said Opus 5.5 "matched Opus 5's quality in about half the turns, time and output tokens, cutting the cost of that workload by 40 to 50%." GitHub Copilot turned it on the same day for Pro+, Max, Business and Enterprise plans, reporting early tests that "resolved tasks comparably to Claude Opus 5 while using significantly fewer steps and tokens."

On our desk

Every Claude Code and Cowork job Patrick runs, from vault clean-ups to the TWS tool stack, is billed by the token, so a model that finishes the same work in fewer steps makes more of those jobs worth automating for a two-person shop.

OpenAI cuts prices with new GPT-6 Sol and Luna

OpenAI released two new API-only models the same day as Opus 5.5: GPT-6 Sol, priced at $2.00 per million input tokens and $10.00 per million output tokens, and GPT-6 Luna, at $0.10 and $0.50. Both are for developers building tools, not for chatting directly. OpenAI says "Luna at max beats GPT-5.6 Sol at medium for 1/10 of the cost," and Sol scored 68.8% on DeepSWE v1.1, a coding benchmark. Cached input reads — reusing text the model has already seen — get discounts of up to 90%.

This is one source so far, so take the specifics lightly. On your desk: a cheap, fast model like Luna is exactly the kind of workhorse that could run repetitive n8n steps without burning real money.

More in AI

OpenRouter's new Batch API runs jobs at half price

Read the brief

OpenRouter, a service that routes API calls to whichever AI model you pick, launched a Batch API where providers "generally charge 50% (and sometimes less) of their normal per-token price" across more than 70 models. Jobs get a 24-hour window to finish, but the median job finishes in about 7 minutes, and 90% land within an hour — the tradeoff is you send a batch and wait instead of getting an instant reply.

On your desk: this is built for exactly the kind of non-urgent bulk work Patrick already runs — vault processing, batch content passes — at roughly half the usual price.

Xiaomi's open model tops the open-weight leaderboard

Read the brief

Xiaomi open-sourced MiMo-V2.6-Pro, an open-weight model — one whose underlying settings anyone can download and run themselves, rather than only reach through an API. It scored 46 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, the highest of any open-weight model so far (Kimi K3 scored 44, GLM-5.3 scored 45; top proprietary models sit around 53). Xiaomi trained it with reinforcement learning in under six days on roughly 750,000 practice trajectories, for about $2.62 million, and it ships with 3D spatial reasoning, multimodal perception, computer-use abilities and a desktop client.

This is one source so far, and the license terms weren't stated. On your desk: not a tool Patrick would run today, but a sign of how fast the gap between open and closed models is closing.

Spirit

Same trip, different brains: three psychedelics, three circuits

The spirit beat was quiet this week, so both items here reach back to early September — the charter allows up to 30 days when there isn't much new. A University of Southern Denmark study, published in Nature Communications, PET-scanned rats given LSD, psilocybin or 2C-B, both during the acute high and a week later. Lead researcher Frederik Gudmundsen found each drug lit up its own separate brain networks: "2C-B mainly affected areas linked to reward, LSD affected areas linked to habits and motivation, while psilocybin stood out by primarily affecting areas involved in emotions." Associate Professor Mikael Palner added, "Each drug appears to affect its own networks, and that is new knowledge."

It's a rat study, so human confirmation is still needed. On your desk: a useful footnote for anyone tracking altered states alongside meditation and remote-viewing work.
