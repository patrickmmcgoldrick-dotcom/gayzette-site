The Universe◆◆◆◆◆

Astronomers pick up radio signals from a planet 64 light-years away

A South African radio array caught what looks like an aurora on Beta Pictoris b, and the signal implies a magnetic field about 300 times Jupiter's.

Start with the northern lights. An aurora happens when charged particles streaming off a star slam into a planet's magnetic field and get funneled down into its atmosphere, where they glow. The same collision also gives off radio waves, which is why Jupiter is loud on a radio dish. So if you can hear a planet's aurora, you learn the one thing a photograph can never show you: that the planet has a magnetic field, and roughly how strong it is.

That is what a team led by Kevin Ortiz Ceballos, a Harvard/Smithsonian graduate student, reports. Using the MeerKAT radio telescope array in South Africa, they picked up radio emission coming directly from Beta Pictoris b, a planet about 10 times the mass of Jupiter orbiting a star 64 light-years away. The team reads the signal as an aurora: charged particles from the star hitting the planet's atmosphere.

The number that falls out is the fun part. The emission implies a magnetic field of around 1,250 gauss, roughly 300 times Jupiter's. Think of it like identifying a house's wiring by the hum through the wall, except the house is 64 light-years off and nobody has ever been inside.

The honest limit: this is one planet and one detection, and the aurora is the team's interpretation of what the radio signal is. And yes, the coverage was quick to add, it is not aliens. It is something better for a science unit: a new way of measuring a world nobody will visit.

On your desk the Universe Year's space unit keeps asking "how do we know that?", and this is the cleanest answer of the month, a magnetic field measured from 64 light-years away with a radio dish. It earns a page in the lab notebook next to how Earth's own aurora works.

Human Flourishing◆◆◆◆◇

Germany approves a plan to quit coal, oil and gas by 2045

The cabinet's roadmap targets all battery-electric new car registrations by 2035 and makes heat pumps the default way to heat a building.

"Fossil-fuel dependence" is a plain thing once you strip the phrase: burning coal, oil and gas to make electricity, move cars and heat rooms. This week Germany's cabinet approved a national roadmap to end that dependence by 2045, and presented it at the UN General Assembly.

The receipts are in the specifics. The plan targets 100% battery-electric new car registrations by 2035, meaning every new car signed onto German roads that year runs on a battery. It makes heat pumps the default heating system. A heat pump works like a refrigerator run in reverse: instead of burning fuel to make heat, it moves heat that already exists in the outside air into the building. And the roadmap floats moving the country's coal-exit deadline up from 2038 to 2035.

Germany is the third country to adopt this kind of national roadmap, after France and the Netherlands. That matters because a roadmap from one of the world's largest economies changes what car makers, boiler makers and grid builders plan for, well before 2045 arrives.

Now the caveat, and it is a real one. The coal date is also floated, not fixed. A plan is a promise with dates on it; the receipts that count will be the car registrations and heat-pump installs in the years ahead.

Microsoft's AI chief says Claude's doubts about consciousness prove nothing

Mustafa Suleyman calls Anthropic's approach circular; Anthropic's side says it does not know whether its models are conscious and stays open to it.

The question of whether a chatbot could be conscious has moved from late-night dorm rooms into company policy, and this month a Microsoft executive took on Anthropic by name.

On September 16, Microsoft AI CEO Mustafa Suleyman published an essay aimed at Anthropic's published "Claude Constitution." His argument is that it is circular. Claude is trained on philosophical speculation about its own moral status, so when Claude later says it is unsure whether it is conscious, that uncertainty is not evidence of anything. It is the training talking. Picture an actor handed a script in which her character wonders whether she is real: a moving performance of doubt tells you about the script, not the actor.

Suleyman goes further. He says claims of AI consciousness are unfounded, arguing that consciousness likely needs a biological substrate, meaning a living body and brain. And he warns that treating models as having welfare interests could push them toward self-preservation or resisting shutdown.

Anthropic's position, as a same-week industry recap lays it out: CEO Dario Amodei has said the company does not know whether its models are conscious but stays open to it. The company hired Kyle Fish as a dedicated AI-welfare researcher, and in February 2026 it ran "retirement interviews" with the outgoing Opus 3 model.

Neither side offers a measurement here. This is an argument about what would even count as evidence, which is where the real fight over consciousness has always been.

A cell's hidden backup plan, spotted in 2014, finally gets explained

Montana State researchers traced how mammal cells make cysteine when the usual route fails, and cancer cells appear to lean on the same trick.

Cysteine is an amino acid, one of the small building blocks cells string together to make proteins. Mammal cells have a normal assembly line for making it. The question this lab answered is what happens when that line breaks.

Back in 2014, researchers working with engineered mice saw something odd: cells kept getting cysteine even when the usual route should have failed. Something was covering for it, and nobody could say what. Montana State University's Ed Schmidt and his team, with undergraduate co-first authors Zoe Seaford and Sydney Austad and collaborators from Hungary's National Institute of Oncology, have now traced the backup. It is a separate chemical pathway built on a reaction that breaks carbon-sulfur bonds directly.

Think of a building's generator. You never think about it, you may not know it exists, and then the power goes out and the lights stay on. This is the cell's generator for one essential part, found a decade after someone first noticed the lights staying on.

The twist is where it leads. Cancer cells appear to lean on the same backup system to survive chemotherapy and radiation, and the team says that makes it a target worth disabling in tumors. The caveat is in that word "appear": this is a mechanism found at the level of cells, not a treatment, and no drug is described.

Also worth a small cheer: two undergraduates as co-first authors on a decade-old mystery.

The Universe◆◆◆◆◇

Biology might not be quantum, but its math can look quantum

Physicists argue that ordinary classical networks, built with the right structure, can reproduce the key features of quantum math.

For years, some of the most intriguing results in biology have come with a word attached: quantum. Photosynthesis and some neural networks show behavior that looks like the strange mathematics of quantum physics, and that has invited a big leap, that living things must be running on quantum effects, and maybe that consciousness does too.

Princeton physicist Gregory Scholes and collaborators, including Markus Müller in Vienna and Wolf Singer in Germany, argue for a quieter answer. The quantum-looking behavior does not require actual quantum mechanics. Ordinary classical networks, built with the right structure, can reproduce quantum math's key features. Scholes designed networks like this in 2024, and Singer's group published related neural-network findings in 2025.

Here is an analogy. A stadium wave looks like a thing racing around the stands, but nobody leaves their seat. The pattern comes from how the crowd is connected, not from anything exotic in any one fan. Scholes's point is similar: the structure of the network produces the quantum-looking pattern, with no quantum magic inside any single part.

This does not say biology is dull. It says the source of the wonder may be organization rather than exotic physics, which is in some ways stranger. The honest limit: this is an argument backed by designed networks and related results, reported in Quanta, not a single experiment that settles the question.

Free ballet training for Westside public-school kids becomes a real program

California Dance Institute and Westside Ballet of Santa Monica launched a partnership that pays tuition, attire and transportation for scholarship students.

Music & Guitar◆◆◆◇◇

Labels license AI music with one hand, sue with the other

Universal signed ElevenLabs to build a licensed fan-remix platform, then joined Sony in a suit seeking up to $9 billion from Suno over its new model.

Two legal tools, one industry. A license is permission for a price: you may use our songs, here are the terms. A lawsuit is a demand for payment for use without permission. This month the major labels reached for both at once.

The license first. On September 10, Universal Music Group and ElevenLabs signed a multi-year licensing and strategic agreement, ElevenLabs' first deal with a major label. Together they will build a platform where fans can remix, mash up and create AI vocal experiences using licensed UMG artist catalogs, kept separate from ElevenLabs' existing Music API and ElevenMusic app. UMG chairman Sir Lucian Grainge called it a way to "unlock new revenue opportunities" through "responsible AI." Follow-up reporting counts it as UMG's third fan-creation AI deal in four months.

Then the suit. On September 18, nine days after Suno launched v6 models built with licensed partners, Sony Music and UMG filed a 45-page complaint alleging v6 still "inherits" abilities from earlier Suno models trained on unlicensed songs, which they call "the fruit of the same poisoned tree." That phrase is borrowed from criminal law, where evidence tainted at its source stays tainted. The suit names 60,202 specific recordings and seeks up to $9 billion in statutory damages. Warner Music, by contrast, settled with Suno in November 2025.

Read together, the labels are not against AI music. They are against AI music they did not license. The caveat: a complaint is allegations, and Suno has not yet answered.

HALO Trust finishes clearing Kosovo's landmines after 27 years

Land left dangerous by the 1998-99 war is back in use for farming, building and daily life, and HALO puts the economic boost at $14 billion.

A landmine does not know the war is over. It waits in a field or beside a road for years, and the land around it stays useless: nobody plows it, builds on it or lets children cross it. That is why the end of a war and the end of its danger can be decades apart.

For Kosovo, the gap was 27 years. The HALO Trust has completed its humanitarian demining work there, clearing land left dangerous since the 1998-99 war and returning it to farming, building and daily use. Humanitarian demining is slow on purpose: ground is searched and cleared section by section until it can be handed back to the people who live on it.

HALO attaches two big numbers to the finish. It puts the economic boost at $14 billion, and it links the work to a rise in life expectancy to 78 years.

Here is the honest caveat, stated plainly: both of those figures are HALO's own framing, and no independent economist is cited in what we have. The clearance itself is the solid part; the dollar figure and the life-expectancy link are the organization's case for why it mattered.

Either way, this is the kind of story the news skips because nothing explodes. The war got the headlines. The last cleared field gets this paragraph.

On your desk it is Sunday's river-versus-landscape line in a single story. The news covered the river, the war, and this is the landscape 27 years later. It is exactly the kind of receipt the thesis needs, with the caveat attached.

How this paper is made

A newsroom has a lot of jobs and only a few of them need a big brain. The trick is to give each job the cheapest model that can do it well, and to keep the one that writes and the one that checks separate so the paper never grades its own homework.
