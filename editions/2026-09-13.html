The Universe◆◆◆◆◆

Ten thousand AI agents proved a million-dollar math problem

OpenAI ran a swarm of autonomous agents for 88 hours and came back with a Millennium Prize answer that a machine then checked line by line.

Two definitions first, because the whole thing turns on them. The Navier-Stokes equations are the math that describes how fluids move, the rules behind smoke curling off a match and water shoving past a bridge piling. A singularity, or a "blow-up," is the moment those equations stop describing anything at all, when a quantity like the speed of the fluid runs to infinity at a point and the math throws up its hands. For twenty-five years, one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a $1 million purse, has asked whether blow-up can actually happen in three-dimensional fluid flow.

It can. Quanta Magazine reported on September 8 that OpenAI ran roughly 10,000 autonomous AI agents for 88 hours and produced a proof that it can.

Here is the part that makes this a receipt rather than a press release. The proof was then translated into Lean, a verification language in which every step of an argument has to be spelled out in a form a computer can check, and the machine checked it for 17 more hours. Lean does not care who wrote the argument or how confident they sound. It either closes or it does not. This one closed.

And a human team got there too. Mathematicians Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge independently produced blow-up proofs in the same window, for the Euler equations and an easier Navier-Stokes variant rather than the identical full problem, and both efforts built on the same underlying strategy from Diego Cordoba and Luis Martinez-Zoroa. Two groups, two routes, one answer.

The caveat: this resolves one of the seven Millennium problems, not the version of the fluid question that mathematicians consider the crown jewel, and the corroboration detail comes from the same Quanta piece rather than two independent write-ups.

On your desk Universe Year is math plus biology, and this is the cleanest possible demonstration of what your math half is actually for. A twenty-five-year-old open question fell to a swarm of agents, and then a proof checker with no opinions confirmed it. That is the shape of evidence you keep asking De Stefano's claims to have.

China's solar capacity just passed its coal capacity

By one million kilowatts out of 1.286 billion, the country that burns the most coal on Earth now has more solar than coal installed.

Installed capacity is the number to understand here. It means the maximum power a country's plants could put out if everything ran flat out at once, measured in kilowatts. It is not the same as the electricity actually generated, because a solar panel does nothing at midnight and a coal plant can burn around the clock. Think of it as the size of the kitchen rather than the number of meals served.

China's National Energy Administration confirmed that as of the end of July 2026, installed photovoltaic capacity hit 1.286 billion kilowatts, edging past coal-fired capacity at 1.285 billion kilowatts. The margin is one million kilowatts, which on a base that size is a rounding error. Euronews reported the crossover on September 1.

The margin is not the story. The direction is. This is the first time in history that solar has been the single largest power-source category by installed capacity in the country that burns more coal than anywhere else on the planet, and capacity gets built years before it gets retired. A coal plant that exists still gets used; a solar fleet this size changes what the marginal new kilowatt looks like for the rest of the decade.

The honest caveat, said plainly: because of that capacity-versus-generation gap, coal still produces far more of China's actual electricity than solar does. This is a crossover in what has been built, not in what is currently being burned. The second number follows the first, but it follows it with a lag.

Still, the chart crossed. That happened, it has a date, and it has a source.

On your desk your whole thesis is that the world is getting more capable and nobody puts it on the front page. This is the chart-not-vibes version of it, and it is the kind of receipt you can hand to the friend who thinks optimism is a personality disorder.

Early reports: near-death experiences may share one shape worldwide

A 15-year cardiac-arrest study across four countries found the same structural elements in survivors' accounts regardless of nationality or religion.

AWARE II is a study run out of NYU Langone by Dr. Sam Parnia that does something most consciousness research cannot: it watches people during clinical death. Cardiac arrest is the cleanest natural experiment available, because the heart stops, blood stops reaching the brain, and the person is either resuscitated or not. Over more than 15 years, the study tracked 550 cardiac-arrest survivors across the United States, United Kingdom, Canada and Austria.

About a quarter of them recalled an experience from the period when they were clinically dead. The interesting finding is not that they recalled something. It is what the recollections had in common. Reported as strikingly consistent structural elements: a sense of detachment from the body, an awareness of the resuscitation happening, a boundary of some kind, and encounters with deceased relatives. That consistency held regardless of the survivor's nationality or religious background, which is the opposite of what you would expect if these were culturally scripted stories people tell afterward.

There is a second result with practical teeth. Survivors whose experiences were clinically acknowledged, rather than waved off as "just a dream," fared better psychologically than those whose accounts were dismissed. That is a finding about how clinicians should talk to patients, and it stands on its own regardless of what the experience is.

Now the honest part, and it matters. This comes to us through a single secondary report of the underlying Resuscitation Plus paper, so treat the specifics as early reporting rather than settled. The study itself states it cannot rule out residual brain activity as the explanation. A shared structure is not proof of a shared destination; it may just mean brains fail in similar ways.

On your desk the remote-viewing curriculum is built on honest scoring, and this is the same posture applied to the biggest question in the beat. A large, cross-cultural dataset with the authors naming their own strongest alternative explanation is exactly the kind of source worth putting next to your De Stefano notes.

Consciousness & The Nonphysical◆◆◆◆◇

An out-of-body trial failed its test and found something else

UVA researchers got chance-level results on the target they set out to test, then two participants described what the scientists were doing behind closed doors.

The University of Virginia's Division of Perceptual Studies runs the kind of experiment that is easy to do badly. The protocol here was tight: 21 Brazilian participants, each attempting an out-of-body experience and then trying to identify a random object displayed on a laptop in a different room. Pre-registered target, one right answer, no wiggle room about what counts as a hit.

Accuracy was no better than chance. That is the headline result, and the team published it as such, which is worth pausing on. A null result is a study that found nothing, and in this field nulls have a habit of quietly not getting written up. This one did.

Then the part they did not plan for. Two participants, unprompted, described the researchers' locations and what they were doing behind closed doors. That was not the target, not the hypothesis, and not something the protocol was designed to score. The team reported it anyway as an unplanned finding, published in the journal Explore, with the researchers named on the paper: Marina Weiler, Damon Abraham, David J.P. Acunzo and Niffe Hermansson.

The caveat is the one the researchers themselves imply by calling it unplanned. An anomaly noticed after the fact, in two people out of 21, with no pre-set criteria for what would have counted as a miss, is a lead and not a result. It is the thing you design the next study around, not the thing you conclude from.

Which is the whole point. This is what the field looks like when it behaves: say what you predicted, report that it failed, and report the odd thing too.

On your desk your CRV curriculum's entire premise is honest scoring, including the sessions that miss. This is a lab modeling that discipline in public, and the Division of Perceptual Studies is now worth a standing spot on your source list next to IRVA.

Brian Henson's Puppet Up! tours for the first time in 15 years

An adults-only improv show with 80-plus Henson puppets sold out Hollywood and is now booking Boston, Philadelphia, DC and New York.

Puppet Up! is Brian Henson's adults-only improvised puppet show, and the staging is the clever part. More than 80 Jim Henson Company puppets are performed live by puppeteers working at floor level, while cameras capture only the puppets and project that feed onto screens above them. The audience gets both at once: the finished shot, and the sweaty humans making it. It is the reveal and the magic trick running in the same room.

After a sold-out Hollywood run, the show is going out on the road for the first time in 15 years. Confirmed fall dates are Boston on September 27, Philadelphia on September 29, Washington DC on September 30, and New York's Tribeca Performing Arts Center on October 2 and 3, hosted by Patrick Bristow.

What makes this a story rather than a listing is the economics it demonstrates. Unscripted adult puppet comedy, with no script to license and no post-production, sold out a residency and is now worth the cost of moving 80 puppets and a crew between four cities in a week. The format is repeatable by design, because improv means the show regenerates its own material every night. That is a very different cost structure from scripted comedy, and it is the reason a 15-year gap can close this fast.

The caveat is scale, not quality: four cities and a handful of dates is a test tour, not a national rollout, and whether it extends past October is not something the announcement says.

A book award now judges on visible human authorship

The getAbstract International Book Award built "who actually wrote this" into its criteria and longlisted ten titles under it.

A longlist is the first public cut in a prize, the stage where a field of submissions narrows to a set of finalists-in-waiting. On August 31, the 26th getAbstract International Book Award for Business Impact revealed its longlist and framed the whole thing around a criterion that did not need stating until recently: that a human wrote the book.

Ten titles are longlisted in the international category, including "What Is Intelligence?" by Blaise Aguera y Arcas and "Creativity in the Age of AI." Rights Director Laura Lesum put the reasoning plainly: "at a time when knowledge is increasingly processed by AI systems, making its origins visible matters more than ever." Winners are announced October 8 at the Frankfurt Book Fair.

The word doing the work in that quote is "visible." Not banned, not detected, not policed. Visible. A prize cannot verify how a manuscript was made, and this one is not pretending it can. What it can do is make provenance part of what it rewards, which turns a fuzzy anxiety into a stated value that authors and publishers can point at.

The caveat: this is one award in one category, and a criterion is not an enforcement mechanism. Nobody is auditing drafts.

But it is a publishing institution deciding, on the record, that the origin of a book is part of the book's worth. That is the kind of quiet institutional signal that tends to precede a norm rather than follow one, and ghostwriting sits in an interesting spot next to it: a discipline that is, by definition, a human voice standing behind somebody else's page.

The Operator◆◆◆◇◇

Squarespace email campaigns get tagging, filters and conditions

Three additions let the same automation treat a waitlisted customer and a paying one differently, without anyone sorting a list by hand.

Squarespace's August product update, posted August 25, added three things to Email Campaigns that together change what the tool is for.

Auto-Tagging labels customers automatically based on what they do, like making a purchase or filling out a form. A tag is just a sticky note on a contact record, but an automatic one means the sorting happens at the moment of the behavior rather than in a spreadsheet later. Trigger Filters let you segment an audience by the data people entered in a form response, so the answers to your intake questions become the basis for who gets what. Conditions split an automation into branches, so different customers walk different follow-up paths from the same starting point. The same update added downloadable, branded invoice PDFs.

None of these is new as a concept; dedicated email platforms have had branching for years. What is new is that they are sitting inside the website builder, one layer away from the form and the checkout that generate the behavior in the first place. The value of that is not features, it is fewer joints. Every integration between two systems is a place where a contact record goes stale or a tag never arrives, and removing a joint removes a class of silent failure.

The caveat: a branching automation is only as good as the segments you define, and a badly designed condition just delivers the wrong email faster.

For a small operation, that is still the difference between treating everyone identically and treating people like you noticed what they did.

On your desk Squarespace is the front door of Travis Wall Studio, and premium positioning lives or dies on whether a waitlisted student and an enrolled one get the same email. Conditions plus Trigger Filters is the piece that makes scarcity-based funnels run without you hand-sorting anybody.

The AI Builder◆◆◆◆◇

OpenAI unbundled its coding agent and sold it as an API

The Agents API exposes the same session, sandbox and subagent machinery behind Codex, with no platform fee on top of tokens.

A harness is the unglamorous scaffolding around a model that makes it useful for long jobs: the thing that decides what stays in memory, which tools get offered, where the code actually runs, and what happens when a task takes nine hours instead of nine seconds. Building one is most of the work in building an agent. Until now, if you wanted OpenAI's harness, you used their product; you could not rent the plumbing.

On September 10, OpenAI released the Agents API in public beta. It exposes the same agent, environment and session infrastructure that powers Codex as a standalone service. Three pieces matter for a solo builder. Automatic context compaction means the agent trims its own working memory as a job runs long, instead of hitting a wall. On-demand tool search means it fetches a tool's definition only when it needs it, rather than paying tokens to carry every tool description in every request. And native multi-agent delegation means subagents run in parallel, each with its own context, so one long task splits into several short ones.

Sandboxes, the isolated machines where the agent's code actually runs, can be hosted by OpenAI or by nine partner platforms including Cloudflare, Vercel, DigitalOcean and E2B. There is no platform fee. You pay for tokens and tool usage, full stop.

The caveat is the phrase "public beta," which in this industry means the interface will change and you should not wire anything load-bearing to it yet without expecting to rewire it.

On your desk you already orchestrate with n8n plus Claude Code, and this is the first credible rented alternative for the long-running, multi-step builds. Worth a weekend test against the repeatable-client-site experiment before you decide whether to keep hand-wiring the harness yourself.

The AI Builder◆◆◆◆◇

A coding model matched the frontier at 64 percent less cost

Cognition's SWE-2 scores within a point of Claude Fable 5.1 on a hard coding benchmark while charging a fraction of the price.

The useful way to read a model release is as two numbers that have to move in opposite directions: how well it does the work, and what the work costs. Most releases move one. This one moved both.

Cognition released SWE-2 on September 10, post-trained from Kimi K3, a base model with 2.8 trillion parameters. Parameters are the tunable weights inside a model, roughly a measure of raw capacity, the way engine displacement is a rough measure of an engine. Post-training is the specialization pass on top of that base, where a general model gets taught to do one job well.

The scores: 50.0% on FrontierCode 1.1 Main, against 50.9% for Claude Fable 5.1. That is within a single point of a frontier model, at 64% less cost. On Terminal-Bench 2.1, which tests whether a model can actually operate a command line rather than just write code that looks right, it hits 92.8%. Cognition also reports it needs 58% fewer turns than its predecessor SWE-1.7 to land a real edit, which is the number a solo builder feels most directly, because every turn is a round trip you sit through.

The caveat, and it is a standing one for the whole category: these are the vendor's own benchmark disclosures on benchmarks the vendor selected. Cognition publishes them in more detail than most, which is a point in their favor, but a benchmark is not your codebase.

The trend underneath is the real receipt. The cost floor for agentic coding keeps dropping while capability holds flat or climbs.

On your desk your Model Ladder rule says put the cheap model on the routine rung and save the expensive thinking for when it earns its keep. SWE-2 is a new candidate for that middle rung, and cheap enough that the agent-first client build stops being a decision and becomes a test you just run.

Suno rebuilt its AI music models on licensed catalog

Three new models trained from scratch on catalog from Warner, BMG and Believe, with rightsholders paid per generation, and every older model retired the same day.

Training data is the whole fight in AI music. A generator learns by ingesting recordings, and the question nobody had a clean answer to was whether those recordings were licensed. Suno's answer, until now, was to argue about it in court.

On September 9, Suno launched v6, the experimental v6-wild, and the free v6-mini, all trained from scratch on catalog licensed from Warner Music Group, BMG and Believe, including its TuneCore arm, with those rightsholders paid per generation. Per generation is the load-bearing phrase. It means the payment is metered to use, not a one-time settlement, which is the structure a licensing chain needs if it is going to be a business rather than a truce. Every prior Suno model was retired the same day, which is a costly way to say the old ones were not defensible.

The gap is real. The training data excludes Universal Music Group and Sony Music Entertainment recordings: neither label is among Suno's three licensing partners, and both labels' infringement suits against Suno continue. So this is the first AI music generator with a real, paid rights chain behind part of its catalog, not all of it.

The practical version for anyone putting generated music under a monetized video: the provenance story is much better than it was last week, and still not complete. Read Suno's own output-license terms before you rely on it, because a clean training set and a clean output license are two different documents.

FDA approves an oral pill for a hard lung cancer subgroup

Sevabertinib produced a 75 percent response rate in previously untreated patients with a specific HER2 mutation.

Targeted therapy means a drug aimed at a specific broken part rather than at fast-dividing cells in general. The broken part here is HER2, also written ERBB2, a gene that codes for a protein sitting on the cell surface that tells the cell when to grow. Certain mutations in the part of that protein called the tyrosine kinase domain jam the signal permanently on, and the cell divides because it has been told to, forever.

On September 9 the FDA granted accelerated approval to sevabertinib, brand name Hyrnuo, for locally advanced or metastatic non-squamous non-small cell lung cancer carrying those HER2 activating mutations. In the SOHO-01 study of 69 previously untreated patients, the objective response rate, meaning the share of patients whose tumors measurably shrank, was 75%. Of those who responded, 73% were still responding at six months and 38% at twelve months.

Two caveats worth holding together. Accelerated approval is a conditional pathway: the FDA clears a drug based on a measure likely to predict benefit, like tumor shrinkage, while confirmatory trials run. It can be withdrawn. And 69 patients is a small trial, which is typical for a narrow genetic subgroup and still a small trial.

What makes it a receipt anyway is the shape of it. Sequence the tumor, find a specific mutation, take a pill twice a day. That is a workflow that barely existed fifteen years ago for lung cancer, and the patients in this subgroup previously had few good options.

On your desk the paper's job is to find the week's evidence that the world got more capable, and precision oncology is where that shows up most reliably. File it next to the CRISPR cholesterol result from Issue 001 as the same story told twice: read the specific defect, aim at the specific defect.

How this paper is made

A newsroom has a lot of jobs and only a few of them need a big brain. The trick is to give each job the cheapest model that can do it well, and to keep the one that writes and the one that checks separate so the paper never grades its own homework. Here is exactly what ran to make this issue, with the token counts. A token is a chunk of text about three quarters of a word; it's the unit these models are metered in.

This was the first edition made entirely by the scheduled task: no live session, no one watching. The one-time vault mapping from Issue 001 did not run again; the editor read your reactions on this page instead (there were none yet).

Scout the beatsSonnet ×9≈843,000One scout per standing beat, in parallel. Each opened every source to confirm date and facts. 47 leads came back; the AI Builder scout worked from your daily digests and only searched to confirm.

Total for this edition: about 1.12 million tokens, over the 900,000 target. Nearly all of the overrun is scouting: nine scouts instead of last week's six, each verifying five to seven leads by opening the pages. Next week's fix is in the charter already: if scouting is heading past budget, keep the five beats with the most 'more' votes. Which means the cheapest way to lower the bill is to tap 'more' on the beats you want.
