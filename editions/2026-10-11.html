Webb traces a far-off radio flash to a surprisingly tiny galaxy

A burst caught on March 4, 2024 left its galaxy about 3 billion years after the Big Bang, and the galaxy is about 1,000 times smaller than expected.

By The Gayzette, with Claude · 2 min read · Wonder ◆◆◆◆◆

Imagine someone flicks a flashlight once from the far side of a city at night, and you have to work out which window it came from. That is the problem with a fast radio burst, or FRB: a sudden, very brief flash of radio waves from deep space. Tracing it home is the hard part.

On March 4, 2024, the MeerTRAP team, using the MeerKAT radio telescope, caught one now called FRB 20240304B. The largest ground-based telescopes saw no galaxy at the spot. Webb did. NASA reported on Oct 8 that the burst sits at a redshift of 2.148. Redshift is how much the expanding universe has stretched light on its way to us, a kind of cosmic odometer, and this reading means the flash left its galaxy about 3 billion years after the Big Bang. It may be the most distant FRB seen so far.

The surprise is the home. It is a small, actively star-forming dwarf galaxy, about 1,000 times less massive than the team expected. That matters for what makes these bursts. One suspect is two neutron stars (the collapsed cores of dead stars) merging; another is a magnetar, a neutron star with an enormous magnetic field. A young, busy little galaxy points toward the magnetar. "Our work suggests that it's very unlikely that this FRB was produced by a merger," said Manisha Caleb of the University of Sydney, who led the study in Science.

On its long trip, the signal also picked up fingerprints of what it passed through, revealing a previously unknown galaxy cluster about 3.5 billion light-years away and the Virgo Cluster about 54 million light-years away. "A fast radio burst is almost like a cosmic flashlight," said J. Xavier Prochaska. The team estimates MeerKAT could pin down several FRBs a year at redshift above 1.0.

The caveat: this is one burst, and the magnetar answer is favored, not proven.

Also leading

Fervo's Utah geothermal plant starts selling power, using oil-field tricks

Cape Station's first phase began selling electricity in late September, and Google has agreed to buy at least 396 megawatts.

Deep underground, the rock is hot all the time: day and night, summer and winter, wind or no wind. Old-style geothermal power needs a lucky spot where that heat already comes with hot water flowing through it, which has kept it stuck in a few places. Enhanced geothermal tries to make its own luck.

Fervo Energy, founded in 2017 in Houston, borrows from the oil industry: its drilling and fracking techniques (fracking means pushing fluid down to crack open paths through rock). Fervo uses them to open channels through hot rock, so it does not need a site where hot water already flows. Think of it as building your own hot spring instead of hunting for one.

MIT Technology Review reported on Oct 6 that the first phase of Fervo's Cape Station in southwestern Utah began selling power in late September. Also in September, Google agreed to buy at least 396 megawatts from Cape Station to fuel a potential data center in Utah, part of a broader Google deal covering up to 3 gigawatts of enhanced geothermal through 2033. (A megawatt is a million watts; a gigawatt is a thousand megawatts.)

The numbers to watch are costs. Fervo's target is about $7,000 per kilowatt of capacity for the first 100 megawatts, about $5,500 per kilowatt for the next 400 megawatts, and a long-term goal of $3,000. The bet is that building the same thing again and again makes each copy cheaper, which is why Fervo has a standard unit, the "GeoBlock," of about 50 megawatts. The company went public in May 2026, raising roughly $2.2 billion, and expects the whole plant in full commercial operation in 2028.

The caveat comes from an outside expert quoted in the article, who said a success in Utah "doesn't mean this tech will easily carry over to another part of the country." A method that works in one patch of rock still has to prove itself in others.

Consciousness & The Nonphysical

People rate AI less conscious than humans, even when it acts identically

Five pre-registered experiments with 1,092 UK participants found people judge minds by what something is, not what it does.

Two words that sound alike do different jobs here. Awareness is taking in what is around you and responding to it. Consciousness is the deeper claim that there is an inner experience, something it is like to be you. A new study asks how ordinary people hand out each one, to humans and to machines.

Read the brief

Researchers including Louis Longin, Jurgis Karpus, Nadine Meertens, Bahador Bahrami and Ophelia Deroy, with LMU Munich named, published the work in the journal Cognition (citation date Sept 22). They ran five experiments in two pre-registered sets, meaning the plan and predictions were written down before any data came in, so nobody can move the goalposts afterward. In total 1,092 UK participants read vignettes, short written scenarios, about human and artificial agents behaving with more or less responsiveness.

The result: at the same level of responsiveness, people gave humans more consciousness than AI. Even the most responsive AI was rated as less conscious than the least responsive human. Awareness worked differently; it rose with responsiveness for both. The paper's abstract puts it neatly: "Consciousness is treated as depending on what an agent is; awareness largely on what an agent does." The title names the habit behind it, essentialism, the belief that a thing's hidden nature counts for more than its behavior.

"People consistently attribute less consciousness to AI than humans, even when their behaviour is identical," said Longin. And Deroy added a thought worth sitting with: "We may be using mental terms to refer to AI, but this may only be because we lack better words."

The caveat: this is about how people perceive AI, not about whether any AI is conscious. The coverage reports no effect sizes, so we cannot say how big the gap is.

A planet may have formed after its star died

Hubble data from 1999, a metal called niobium and four months of TESS watching point to a Jupiter-sized candidate around a white dwarf.

When a star runs out of fuel, it sheds its outer layers and leaves behind a white dwarf, the small, dense leftover core. Planets nearby usually get the worst of that. A new study in Nature Astronomy suggests that around one white dwarf, a planet may have formed afterward, out of the very material the dying star threw off. Astronomers call that a "second-generation" planet: a new house built from the rubble of the old one.

Read the brief

The first clue was old data. Hubble observed the white dwarf HS 0209+0832 in 1999, and its light carried roughly 100 unidentified spectral features. A spectrum is light split into its colors, like a rainbow; each element absorbs particular colors, leaving dark lines like a barcode. A team led by Jamie Williams of the University of Warwick found that one element, niobium, explains many of those mystery lines. The FUSE telescope had also shown strong niobium. "The presence of niobium is a signpost of these 'death' throes," said Nicholas Stone of UW-Madison. Co-author Boris Gänsicke said he was "truly gobsmacked" that niobium had not been reported in any other white dwarf they had analyzed.

The second clue came from TESS, NASA's planet-hunting satellite, which watched the star for four months and saw repeating dips in its light, the sign of something passing in front. The best explanation is a roughly Jupiter-sized candidate about 3.7 million miles (6 million km) from the star, losing its atmosphere. "If the second-generation planet is there, I think it is likely to survive," Williams said.

The caveat: it is a candidate, not a confirmed planet. Still, it is a lovely example of science correcting its own blind spots. Old Hubble and FUSE data, read again with fresh eyes, helped point to a possible new world.

One extra oxygen atom may explain why RNA came before DNA

Buffalo chemists found RNA forms droplets about 10°C lower than matching DNA, and traced it to one small group on RNA's sugar.

Your cells run on two related molecules. DNA is the archive, the master copy of the instructions. RNA is the working copy that cells read from. One long-standing idea about the origin of life, called the RNA world, says RNA came first and DNA took over the archive later. A new study asks a small question with a big shadow: how much does one atom matter?

Read the brief

The two molecules differ in their sugar, the backbone piece each letter hangs on. RNA's sugar carries a 2'-hydroxyl group (say "two-prime hydroxyl"), which amounts to one extra oxygen atom per sugar unit. Chemists at the University at Buffalo, working with Princeton, reported in Nature Communications that this tiny difference changes how the molecules behave in a crowd.

RNA forms droplets, called condensates, at temperatures about 10°C lower than matching single-stranded DNA. Picture oil beading up in vinegar: molecules gathering into droplets instead of staying evenly spread. RNA's droplets also more readily turn gel-like, closer to jelly than liquid. The team traced it to that extra oxygen-containing group, which binds magnesium ions more strongly and holds less water. When they chemically capped it with a modification called 2'-OMe, condensate formation dropped. "This single oxygen-containing group on RNA's sugar has a surprisingly powerful effect," said Gable Wadsworth. Priya Banerjee pointed to "how remarkably small changes in molecular chemistry can control the emergence of much larger" structures.

The work is framed as a test of RNA-world theory, and it was supported by NIH, NSF and the Hypothesis Fund. The caveat: this is a lab result about how molecules clump, not a replay of early Earth. It offers one physical reason RNA might have had an edge, not the whole origin story. But it is a good reminder that in chemistry, a single atom can be the difference between a puddle and a structure.

The Australian Ballet brings Wheeldon's 'Oscar' to New York

The company's first US visit in 15 years, a story ballet about Oscar Wilde's imprisonment, with a 48-piece orchestra playing live.

A story ballet has a strange job: tell a plot with no words at all. Everything the audience needs, who loves whom, who is in danger, what was lost, has to come through bodies, music, costumes and light. Christopher Wheeldon, who moves between ballet and Broadway, took on a hard one with Oscar, about Oscar Wilde's imprisonment.

Read the brief

The Australian Ballet opened Oscar at New York City Center on Oct 9, the company's first US visit in 15 years. The ballet premiered in 2024 and is set in 1895 England. Joby Talbot's score was played live by the 48-piece Orchestra of St. Luke's, and Jean-Marc Puissant designed both the stage and the costumes. Jarryd Madden danced Wilde, Jill Ogai the Nightingale, Jett Ramsay Robbie Ross and Adam Elmes Lord Alfred "Bosie." In a story ballet, the solo is often where a character stops being described and starts being known; take it away and the lead can end up carried by the plot instead of carrying it.

The caveat: the New York run ends Oct 11 (today), so this is a report, not a ticket tip, and it is one critic's view. But the review reads like a checklist any choreographer building a narrative piece could hold up to their own work.

Comedy, Puppetry & TV

YouTube will let creators test three cuts of a Short

Plus dynamic thumbnails and Ask Studio, an AI that gives feedback on a draft video before it goes up.

A/B testing is the oldest trick in advertising made easy: show version A to some people and version B to others, then keep whichever works better. Like testing two punchlines on two different nights, except the audience is split for you and the scorecard is automatic. YouTube already let creators test titles and thumbnails this way. At its Made on YouTube announcements, reported by TechCrunch on Sept 23, it said that starting in 2027 creators can upload and test three different cuts of a Short.

Read the brief

Two other tools came in the same announcement. Dynamic thumbnails let a creator upload several thumbnails and have YouTube choose which to show. Ask Studio lets creators share a draft video with AI for feedback based on their past content and comments; it suggests alternative titles and thumbnails, analyzes channel analytics, and is expanding to YouTube's mobile apps (no date given). Live auto-dubbing, which translates speech in real time, is to be piloted early next year. There is also a "Live showdown" for co-hosted streams; as YouTube Live's Barbara MacDonald put it, "Co-hosts can kick off friendly head-to-head matchups."

Two numbers explain where YouTube thinks attention is going. More than half of the top 100 YouTube creators name TV, the "living room," as their most-watched screen. And microdramas, very short serialized stories, passed 6.5 billion views in the first half of 2026, with watch time up 50% year over year and TV views up 90%.

The caveat: the three-cut test is a 2027 feature, not something to use this month, and the coverage comes from TechCrunch rather than YouTube's own post. For a small character-comedy channel, though, a free way to learn which edit actually lands is a gift.

The Operator

Stripe now lets subscriptions pause and resume on demand

The 2026-09-30 "Endive" API release also makes trial offers generally available and explains why an invoice can't be collected.

Stripe, the payments service the studio bills through, ships changes in dated API versions. An API is the menu of commands your software can send to Stripe; a version is a frozen snapshot of that menu, so your code keeps working until you choose to move up. The newest one, dated 2026-09-30 and nicknamed "Endive," has a few lines a membership business will like.

Read the brief

The headline item, per Stripe's changelog: "Adds the ability to pause and resume Subscriptions on demand." Think of a gym membership freeze: the member steps away, nothing is cancelled, and billing picks up again when they come back. Pausing and resuming are now built-in commands rather than something to wire up yourself.

Second, Stripe "Makes trial offers generally available." Generally available means out of testing and ready for everyone to use. Third, it adds support for scheduling a Subscription cancellation as part of a pending update, so a cancellation can be lined up as part of a change that is already waiting to happen. Fourth, it adds a detailed status that explains why an Invoice is uncollectable, which turns a vague "didn't go through" into a reason you can act on.

All four of those Billing items are marked non-breaking, which means existing code should keep working. One item in the same section is marked breaking: Stripe "Unifies the billing cycle anchor format across Subscriptions and Invoices." The billing cycle anchor is the date a subscription's billing repeats from, and if your code reads that field, it needs a look before you upgrade. The page also points developers to the Stripe CLI (v1.43.3 or newer) and a `stripe agent setup` command.

The caveat: accounts move to new API versions on their own upgrade timeline, so none of this switches on by itself.

Travis Wall Studio bills members through Stripe, and pause-and-resume plus trial offers map directly to a "freeze my membership" or free-trial-week flow without custom code in n8n.

Claude Max and Team plans now come with monthly API credits

Max 5x gets $100 a month and Max 20x gets $200, but you have to claim them, and they don't cover Claude Code.

There are two ways to pay for Claude. A subscription covers the chat apps. The API (the programmatic doorway that scripts, automations and agents use) has been billed separately, by the token, like a utility meter. Anthropic's Oct 7 platform release notes say that gap just narrowed: Max and Team plans now include monthly API credits.

Read the brief

The amounts, from Anthropic's credits page: Max 5x gets $100 a month and Max 20x gets $200 a month. On Team plans, Standard seats get $20 and Premium seats $100, pooled across the team and capped at $500 a month; the page's example is 3 Standard plus 2 Premium seats for $260. Free, Pro and Enterprise plans are not eligible.

What the credits cover: the Claude API, Claude Managed Agents, the Claude Agent SDK and the Playground. What they do not cover: Claude Code, extra usage in the Claude apps, and Claude through Bedrock, Google Cloud, AWS or Foundry. They also do not change your usage limits.

The fine print matters. Credits are not automatic. Think of it as a monthly gift card that refreshes each billing cycle, so it pays to have something running on it.

The caveat: the credits page itself carries no date, so the Oct 7 release note is the timestamp. And if most of your building happens in Claude Code, this particular perk does not touch that bill.

Music & Guitar

Italy's consumer watchdog says Suno's terms may be unfair

The AGCM flagged account rules, a sweeping copyright license and US-only arbitration; fines for unfair terms run from €5,000 to €10 million.

Terms of service are the contract you click "agree" on without reading. Consumer regulators exist partly to read them for you. On Tuesday, Oct 6, Italy's Competition Authority, the AGCM, said Suno's consumer terms "may be unfair pursuant to Article 33 of the Consumer Code," according to Music Business Worldwide.

Read the brief

The list of concerns is worth reading as a user of the tool. The AGCM points to Suno's right to change the contract, the service and prices on its own; to suspend or end accounts without notice; hidden additional terms; and very broad limits on Suno's liability. It also says the copyright license users grant is "insufficiently defined" and requires waiving moral rights, the personal rights an author keeps over their work, which the authority says runs contrary to Italian copyright law (Law 633/1941). And disputes go to individual arbitration in the US: a private referee hears each user's case one at a time, with no class actions and exclusive jurisdiction in Massachusetts.

What happens next: a public consultation is due "over the next few weeks." The AGCM has had power since 2023 to fine companies for unfair terms, from €5,000 to €10 million. Suno's current terms took effect Sept 3, after being published Aug 10. The article included no response from Suno.

The caveat: "may be unfair" is the opening of a process, not a ruling, and Italy's view covers Italian consumer law. But it is a useful prompt. If you make music with a tool, the question of who owns and controls the result is written down somewhere, and now a regulator is reading it closely.

Human Flourishing

New York pedestrian deaths hit their lowest since tracking began in 1910

67 people walking were killed in the first nine months of 2026, down 15% from 2025; e-bike and e-scooter deaths went the other way.

Some progress is loud. A lot of it is a number that quietly gets smaller. On Oct 2, New York City's Department of Transportation released one of those: 67 pedestrian traffic deaths from January through September 2026.

Read the brief

That is the lowest for any January-to-September stretch since the city began tracking in 1910, and down 15% from the same period in 2025. The monthly numbers show it too: 15 deaths this September against 23 a year earlier, and 17 in August against 21. Overall traffic deaths were the second-lowest since 1910.

"Traffic deaths are not inevitable; they are preventable tragedies," said DOT Commissioner Mike Flynn. That line is the whole idea behind this kind of work. Deaths on the street are treated less like weather and more like a design problem, something you can change with how roads, crossings and speeds are set up.

Now the honest counterweight, which DOT's own data shows. Not every group is safer. E-bike rider deaths rose to 17, up 29%, and e-scooter rider deaths rose to 11, up 54%. About 40% of those involved illegal devices. So the city's walkers had a record year while a newer group of riders had a worse one.

Flynn's second line holds both halves: "While traffic fatalities are at record lows this year, we won't let up because one fatality is one too many." The caveat: nine months is not a full year, and one good stretch is not yet a trend. But a 116-year low is a receipt worth putting on the fridge.

How this paper is made

A newsroom has a lot of jobs and only a few of them need a big brain. The trick is to give each job the cheapest model that can do it well, and to keep the one that writes and the one that checks separate so the paper never grades its own homework.
