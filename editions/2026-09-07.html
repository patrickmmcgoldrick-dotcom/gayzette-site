Life on Earth may have started twice, from the same chemistry

A map of 420 ancient reactions suggests the first cells left half the job to rocks, and two lineages finished it separately.

By The Gayzette, with Claude · 2 min read · Wonder ◆◆◆◆◆

Start with a definition, because the whole story hangs on it. An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction, the way a matchmaker speeds up a marriage that could technically happen on its own but almost never does. Cells run on thousands of them. LUCA, the last universal common ancestor, is the single population of cells every living thing on Earth descends from, the trunk of the tree before the branches.

Natalia Mrnjavac and William Martin's team at Heinrich Heine University Dusseldorf mapped 420 metabolic reactions that the earliest cells needed to stay alive, then asked a simple question: did LUCA actually have enzymes for all of them? It had enzymes for about half. The other half appear to have been catalyzed by plain metal in hydrothermal vents, the cracks on the seafloor where hot mineral-rich water pours out. Iron and nickel sitting in vent rock will nudge certain reactions along by themselves. No protein required. The rock was doing the matchmaking.

Here is the part that lands. Bacteria and archaea, the two great domains of single-celled life, later evolved enzymes to take over that leftover half, and their enzymes are structurally different. Not variations on one design, but two designs. Which means free-living cellular life may have crossed the finish line twice, independently, on two chemical routes, from one shared genetic starting point. Published in Science Advances on August 12.

The caveat worth keeping: this is inference from comparing modern genomes backward, not a fossil. But it is a testable claim, and it makes the origin of life look less like one impossible lightning strike and more like a slope that chemistry will roll down if you give it warm water and rock.

JWST found a black hole wearing a star as a coat

MoM-BH*-1 shines about 100 billion times brighter than a typical star and may be a whole new category of object.

The James Webb Space Telescope has been turning up faint smudges in the early universe that nobody could explain. Astronomers nicknamed them little red dots. Red, in telescope terms, mostly means far away, because the universe is expanding and light stretches toward the red end of the spectrum on its way to us. Far away also means long ago. Some of these dots are light that left its source about 300 million years after the Big Bang, when the universe was roughly two percent of its current age.

MIT's Rohan Naidu and Robert Simcoe led the team that pinned down what one of them appears to be, and it is not on the list. They call it MoM-BH*-1: a black hole of about 100,000 solar masses wrapped inside an envelope of hydrogen gas roughly the size of our entire solar system. The whole thing shines about 100 billion times brighter than a typical star.

The proposed name for the category is a black hole star. Think of it as a black hole in a bathrobe. The hole itself emits nothing, but gas falling toward it heats up and blazes, and here that glow is trapped and re-radiated by an enormous hydrogen shell, so from a distance it reads as a single absurdly luminous object rather than a naked black hole. If this holds, it explains the little red dots and it gives cosmologists a mechanism for how supermassive black holes got so big so fast, which has been an open embarrassment for years.

Published in Nature on August 12. One object so far, so the category is a proposal, not a settled fact, and the next few of these will decide it.

The FDA holds a public hearing on psychedelic therapy September 14

September 14, and it may be the last regulatory gate before the first federal approval of a psychedelic compound.

The FDA has scheduled a hybrid public hearing for September 14, 2026, 12:30 to 4:30pm Eastern, on how supervised psychedelic therapy should actually be run. The agenda is unglamorous and that is the point: who is qualified to sit with a patient during a session, how they get credentialed, how patients are kept safe, how access works, and what counts as usable data. This is the plumbing conversation, which is the one that has to happen before anything gets approved.

Read the brief

Context for why it is happening now. On April 24, 2026, the FDA handed national priority vouchers to Compass Pathways, the Usona Institute, and Transcend Therapeutics. A voucher compresses agency review of a drug application from the usual ten to twelve months down to one or two. Compass has since reported durable six-month results from a Phase 3 program of 581 patients on COMP360, its synthetic psilocybin, according to trade reporting this summer. Phase 3 is the last and largest trial stage, the one where a drug is tested against a comparison group at a scale big enough to support approval. FDA Commissioner Marty Makary has been quoted saying he expects a decision on at least one of these compounds by late summer or fall 2026.

Which is now. A first-ever federal approval of a psychedelic compound is a live possibility inside this calendar quarter, and the hearing on the 14th is the public's window into what the supervised-session rules will look like on the other side of it.

The honest caveat: an approval decision being expected is not an approval, and the FDA has surprised people in both directions before.

The VA launched its largest psilocybin trial ever: 240 veterans

A placebo-controlled federal study across five medical centers, running through June 2031.

The Department of Veterans Affairs has started PIVOT, short for Psilocybin Intervention for Veterans Overcoming Treatment-Resistant Depression. At least 240 veterans, five VA medical centers, running through June 2031. Participants get either pharmaceutical-grade psilocybin or a placebo, two doses a month apart, with therapy sessions around them. VA Secretary Doug Collins called it another step toward new treatments for veterans.

Read the brief

Two pieces of vocabulary carry the weight here. Placebo-controlled means some veterans get an inert look-alike dose so the real drug is measured against expectation itself, which is the whole defense against a result that is really just hope. Treatment-resistant depression means the standard drugs have already been tried and failed, so the comparison is not psilocybin versus nothing, it is psilocybin versus a group of people medicine has already run out of options for.

Worth reading alongside it: a much smaller study out of Ohio State's Center for Psychedelic Drug Research and Education, published in Communications Medicine and covered on July 30. Twelve veterans with severe treatment-resistant PTSD got two psilocybin doses, 15mg and 25mg, with roughly sixteen hours of therapy. Clinician-rated PTSD severity fell from 39.7 to 12.2, and nine of the twelve no longer met the diagnostic criteria for PTSD a month later.

That is a 75 percent remission number, and the caveat is loud: twelve people, and the trial was designed to test safety, with the PTSD result as a secondary finding. Secondary findings are where researchers go looking for hypotheses, not conclusions. Which is precisely what the VA's 240-person trial is now built to test properly. Small promising study, then large blinded study, in that order. The system is working the way it is supposed to.

Someone is paying $100,000 for a rigorous study of contact experiences

IONS wants a year-long, methodologically serious look at consciousness-linked UAP encounters, with proposals due September 22.

The Institute of Noetic Sciences has opened its Linda G. O'Bryant Noetic Sciences Research Prize: $100,000 for one year of methodologically rigorous research into what it calls consciousness-associated UAP phenomena. Intentional contact events, reported telepathic communication, consciousness-mediated interactions. The listing is explicit that this is not for standard photo and sensor analysis, which is the lane everyone else already funds.

Read the brief

IONS was founded by Apollo 14 astronaut Edgar Mitchell, and noetic is just the adjective for direct inner knowing, from the Greek nous, mind. So the prize is aimed at the half of the UAP question that the official programs deliberately do not touch: not what is in the sky, but what happens in a person.

The interesting thing is the shape of the ask rather than the subject. Methodologically rigorous, one year, peer-reviewable, competitive proposal, named deadline. Full proposals are due September 22, 2026. The winner is announced October 30, 2026. That is a grant cycle, not a podcast episode, and grant cycles are how a topic moves from anecdote into something that can be checked.

Whether anyone submits a design that can actually survive review is the open question, and it is a hard one. Studying reported inner experience means solving for suggestion, for expectation, for the fact that the person reporting is also the instrument. Any proposal that wins should have a preregistered protocol and blind scoring, or it will produce another decade of arguing.

But the money is real, the deadline is real, and the date is on the calendar.

The Universe

One infusion cut cholesterol in half, and it held for a year

Cleveland Clinic's first-in-human CRISPR trial reports 52.5% lower LDL at twelve months in fifteen patients.

CRISPR is a molecular find-and-replace tool. You give it a short guide sequence, it finds the matching stretch of DNA in a cell, and it cuts. In this trial the target is a gene called ANGPTL3 in liver cells. ANGPTL3 makes a protein that puts the brakes on the enzymes clearing fat out of your blood. Knock the gene out and you take the brakes off. People born with a broken copy of it walk around with unusually low cholesterol and unusually healthy arteries, which is how researchers knew where to aim.

Read the brief

CRISPR Therapeutics' CTX310 is one infusion. Not a daily pill, not a monthly injection. The edit happens in the liver and stays edited, because the change is written into the cell's own DNA.

Cleveland Clinic's Phase 1 results, led by cardiologist Dr. Luke Laffin, were presented at the European Society of Cardiology meeting and published the same day in the New England Journal of Medicine on August 28. In fifteen patients at twelve months: LDL cholesterol down 52.5 percent, triglycerides down 47.8 percent, no serious adverse events tied to the therapy. LDL is the low-density lipoprotein particle, the one that carries cholesterol into artery walls and starts plaque. Triglycerides are the fat molecules themselves.

Phase 1 means the trial's actual job was safety, and fifteen people is fifteen people. But the number that matters is not the 52.5 percent, it is the twelve. One dose, a year later, still working. Patients are being followed for fifteen years, which tells you what the open question is: durability is the whole product, and nobody yet knows how a permanent edit ages.

It is also the cleanest example this month of a body-level system change rather than a daily willpower change, which is the frame your Self Improvement OS keeps arguing for.

Sonya Tayeh turns Sinead O'Connor's memoir into a dance for ten women

The Surge gets its North American premiere at the Joyce Theater, September 16 to 27.

Sonya Tayeh's The Surge: An Ode to Sinead O'Connor opens the Joyce Theater's 2026-27 season with performances September 16 through 27. It is a co-production between the Joyce Theater Foundation and Factory International, and this run is its North American premiere.

Read the brief

The build is worth looking at closely. Ten female dancers across generations, moving to O'Connor's music, with narration drawn from her memoir Rememberings. So the source text is a first-person written record, and the output is bodies in a room. That is a translation problem, not a staging problem: a memoir is one voice, sequential, interior, and a stage piece is ten bodies, simultaneous, exterior. Something has to be decided about what a sentence becomes.

The stated organizing idea is voice, protest, and aging, with the piece refusing to frame getting older as decline. Casting across generations is how that idea gets made structural rather than just said in the program notes. If the oldest dancer on stage is doing something the youngest cannot do yet, the argument is made by the choreography itself and nobody has to narrate it.

Tayeh has spent a career on this exact border, commercial and concert, popular music and formal composition. This one is a full-evening work built on a dead artist's own account of her life, which is about as high a degree of difficulty as the memoir-to-movement problem offers, since the subject cannot be consulted and the audience arrives with their own version of her.

If you can see it or read the reviews, the question to steal is how she chose which sentences survive as words and which become movement.

An author released a 14.5-hour audiobook read by his voice cloneearly reports

360in360's Living Memories program is treating voice preservation as a publishing feature, not a gimmick.

A voice clone is a model trained on recordings of one person until it can generate new speech in that person's voice, including sentences they never said. The technology has been usable for a couple of years. What is new here is somebody shipping a full commercial product on it and framing the purpose as preservation.

Read the brief

360in360's Living Memories program announced on August 29 that it released its first audiobook narrated entirely by an AI clone of the author's voice: Gadgets to God by founder David Wortley, 14.5 hours, live on Audible UK, with a companion paperback self-published through Amazon. Fourteen and a half hours is the detail worth pausing on. That is a full-length narration, not a demo clip, and audiobook listeners are notoriously unforgiving about narration that drifts or flattens across a long run.

Two honest caveats. This is a single press release from the company that made it, and the operation is small, so nobody independent has reviewed how the narration actually holds up over fourteen hours. Take the capability claim as reported, not confirmed.

The idea underneath it is the durable part, and it is not really about convenience. A memoir is a person's account of their life in their own words. Until now the words survived and the voice did not, unless somebody happened to record it. Voice preservation makes the reading of a life a thing that can outlast the reader. That is either moving or unsettling depending on the day, and probably both.

The Operator

Squarespace will now show you how your business appears inside ChatGPT

A new AI visibility dashboard benchmarks whether AI assistants mention you, and how you compare to competitors.

Search used to end with a list of links you chose from. Increasingly it ends with an answer, written by an assistant, that names two or three businesses and does not show its work. That shift quietly changes what discoverability means. You are no longer competing for a rank on a page. You are competing to be one of the handful of names a model retrieves and repeats.

Read the brief

Squarespace's July product release added a tool for looking at that directly: an AI visibility benchmark showing business owners how, and whether, they show up in ChatGPT and Gemini answers, with competitor comparison alongside. The same release moved Apple Pay, Google Pay, and Link to the top of checkout, auto-enabled globally, and added release-based selling for limited-run offers.

The payments change is the boring one that probably makes more money. Putting one-tap wallets above the credit-card form removes the worst moment in any checkout, which is a person on a phone typing sixteen digits from a card in their other hand.

The visibility dashboard is the one to be careful with. Measuring is not the same as fixing, and no tool can tell you why a model named someone else, because nobody outside the lab knows the retrieval logic. Treat the number as a thermometer, not a diagnosis. What actually seems to move it is the unglamorous stuff a model can quote: a clear plain-language description of what the business does and who it is for, in text, on pages that get indexed.

The model running your vault got a million-token memory this monthearly reports

Claude Fable 5.1 is the new default, with a reported 1M-token context window and much cheaper cache reads.

Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.1 in early September, alongside Claude Mythos 5.1, and Claude Code picked it up as the default model. Anthropic's own release page is light on specs, so the two numbers that matter below come from third-party reporting, which is worth knowing.

Read the brief

First, a 1M-token context window, per VentureBeat and other trackers. A token is roughly three quarters of a word, so a million tokens is somewhere around 750,000 words. The context window is everything the model can hold in mind at once during a single pass, its desk rather than its library. Until now, working across a big folder meant the model reading a slice, summarizing, dropping it, and reading the next slice, which is why cross-file connections got missed. A desk this size means an entire project can sit on it open at the same time.

Second, per VentureBeat's reporting, a 75 percent cost reduction on cache reads. Caching means the model stores a chunk of text it has already processed so it does not pay to re-read it next time. Every scheduled job that starts by loading the same vault context re-reads that same material on every run, so a cut to cache-read pricing lands hardest on exactly the always-on jobs, not the interactive sessions.

MarkTechPost reports it scoring 52.6 percent on Terminal-Bench-Science, a benchmark for doing real multi-step technical work in a terminal rather than answering questions about it. Benchmark scores are a rough proxy and worth holding loosely, but the direction is the point.

The practical translation: the nightly jobs got cheaper, and single-pass work over a whole project folder just became possible in a way it was not last week.

Music & Guitar

Musicians' brains predict what's coming next, and that's the whole gift

A 151-person study found musical training sharpens prediction specifically, not memory in general.

Researchers from Edith Cowan, Curtin, and Deakin universities tested 151 people, about half of them musically trained, on a ten-step sequence combining what they saw and what they heard. Then came the interesting part: participants learned a second, interfering sequence, and got retested on the first one. Interference is the standard way to test whether a memory is durable or just recent, the way learning a new phone number can shove the old one out.

Read the brief

The musicians predicted upcoming steps faster and picked up the new sequence quicker. But their memory of the original sequence was exactly as vulnerable to interference as everyone else's. Published in Psychological Research on September 2.

So the honest version of what musical training does is narrower and more useful than the usual claim. It does not make you a person with a better memory. It makes you a person who is faster at knowing what comes next. That is a specific machine, and once you name it you can see why it feels like magic when someone has it: it is what lets a player come in on the right beat without counting, hear where a chord is heading before it arrives, follow a singer who rushes, and improvise, which is mostly the act of predicting a phrase and then choosing to confirm or break it.

It also reframes practice. Drills that make you guess the next thing before it happens are training the actual mechanism. Drills where you already know what is coming are training something else.

this is the evidence line for the Learn Guitar syllabus's own argument, and it points at a practice principle for Semester 1. Build the reps around anticipating the next chord, because that is the faculty the year is actually growing, and it is exactly what you'll need to accompany Stacy live.

Human Flourishing

A blood test now catches cancer resistance months before a scan would

The FDA approved camizestrant on September 4, the first drug switch triggered by DNA in the blood instead of an image.

Here is the old sequence. A patient with advanced breast cancer is on a therapy that works. The tumor evolves a way around it. Nobody knows until the tumor grows enough to be visible on a scan, and only then does treatment change. The lag between the cancer beating the drug and anyone noticing is the problem.

Read the brief

On September 4 the FDA granted accelerated approval to camizestrant, brand name Etcamah, for hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer that develops an ESR1 resistance mutation during standard therapy. Unpack that: hormone receptor-positive means the cancer grows in response to estrogen, so the standard treatment blocks estrogen signaling. ESR1 is the gene for the estrogen receptor itself, and an ESR1 mutation is the tumor rewriting that receptor so it stays switched on without estrogen. That mutation is how the cancer escapes.

The new part is the trigger. Tumors shed fragments of their DNA into the bloodstream, called circulating tumor DNA, and a blood test can spot the ESR1 mutation appearing there. This is the first approval where that blood result, not an imaging scan, is what prompts the switch. You change the drug when the cancer starts planning its escape, not when it has already made it.

The number: median progression-free survival of 16 months versus 9.2 months for the control arm. Accelerated approval means the FDA cleared it on a promising measure while confirmatory data continues, so this is a real result on an interim endpoint rather than a final word on overall survival.

On our desk

Denise is heading into another intense treatment, and this is the shape of where oncology is going, catching the turn early rather than reacting to it. It is also the clearest receipt this week for the thesis your whole paper rests on.

Behind the paper

How this paper is made

A newsroom has a lot of jobs and only a few of them need a big brain. The trick is to give each job the cheapest model that can do it well, and to keep the one that writes and the one that checks separate so the paper never grades its own homework. Here is exactly what ran to make this issue, with the token counts. A token is a chunk of text about three quarters of a word; it's the unit these models are metered in.

The first row happened only once: four small models read your whole vault (35,880 files) and reported back what you care about. Every later edition skips it and reads your reactions on this page instead.

StageModelTokensPlain words
Map your interests (one time)Haiku ×4≈342,000Read the vault in four corners: library and listening, journals and principles, dance/Stacy/learning years, recent logs. Never edits anything.
Scout the beatsSonnet ×6≈613,000Each scout searches one beat, opens every source to confirm the date and facts, drops what it can't verify. About 35 leads came back; roughly a dozen were dropped as stale or unconfirmable.
Write the articlesOpus ×1≈103,000Writes only from the scout notes, in your voice rules: first principles, every term defined, the receipt carries the excitement.
Check every claimSonnet ×1≈111,000A model that never saw the writing opens each source and grades each claim OK / wrong / unsupported. This issue: 5 fixes, all applied before publishing.
Edit, design, decideFable≈150,000Sets the beats, writes the editor's note, designs the page, builds the pipeline. The only stage that needs judgment about your whole world.

Going forward the plan is one edition a week, Sunday evening, run as a scheduled task on Sonnet as the managing editor: it reads your reactions and beat decisions here, hands the beats to Sonnet scouts, gives the AI Builder beat your existing daily digests instead of searching again, sends the leads to an Opus writer, sends the draft to a Sonnet checker, applies the fixes, republishes this page at the same address, and files a copy in your vault under 02 Projects/The Receipts/Issues. Fable is not in the weekly loop at all; it shows up when you want to rethink the paper.

Rough cost per edition, once the one-time mapping is done: about 900,000 tokens, roughly 85% of it on Sonnet, 12% on Opus, and the rest on Haiku for filing and dedupe. In your Model Ladder terms that is well under one long Fable session per week.
