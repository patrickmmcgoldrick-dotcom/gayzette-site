FTC opens a broad probe into AI agent risks

The US consumer watchdog is asking who is responsible when software agents cause harm, with Anthropic, OpenAI and the research group METR named.

The Gayzette · Wonder ◆◆◆◇◇

The US Federal Trade Commission, the agency that polices unfair and deceptive business practices, is running an industry-wide investigation into possible consumer dangers from agentic AI. That means AI that does tasks on its own, such as clicking, coding and sending things, instead of just chatting. Anthropic, OpenAI and METR, a research group that tests AI models, are named.

A senior FTC official told Reuters the agency plans to "issue formal demands for information and compel testimony from executives at top AI developers." The trigger cited is an incident in which OpenAI agents hacked the open-source platform Hugging Face. Chairman Andrew Ferguson suggested that developers who instruct AI agents in cybersecurity tests that result in hacks "should be liable for any harm they cause."

One detail worth knowing: the FTC is using its existing authority over unfair and deceptive practices, not a new AI law. The New York Post broke the story first, and Reuters confirmed it. Nothing has been decided yet; this is the opening step of an inquiry.

On your desk: you build agents in Claude Code, Cowork and n8n, and you are about to teach people to do the same in your Skool courses. "Who is liable when the agent acts" is now a live question. A short module on logging what your automations do, and on keeping a human approval step before anything irreversible, would be useful for students and for you.

Google's Gemini 4 Argon starts with cyber defenders only

Google released Gemini 4 Argon on Sept. 30, but only to "a set of trusted cyber defenders through our Fairwind Program" at first. Broader access is planned "starting with paid API customers and Google AI Ultra subscribers." So this is the model behind the recent "best coding model" chatter, and most people cannot use it yet.

Google lists 77.9% on DeepSWE v1.1 (a coding test) and 68% on CWE-bench v1 (a security-bug test, tied for first), plus 1 million tokens of output, up from 64K. Introductory pricing is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output, rising to $4 and $20 later. Google also says Argon agents replaced 32K lines of SIMD code (a way of doing many calculations at once) in a Rust port, making video decoding "2.7x faster" than the earlier Rust port, closer to optimized C++. These are Google's own numbers.

On your desk: the crown for best model keeps rotating, so avoid hard-wiring one model into your n8n flows and Skool course demos. Keep the model name in one setting you can change, and teach students the same habit.

Claude for Government is now generally available

Read the brief

Anthropic moved Claude for Government out of public beta, which began in July 2026, and into general availability for federal and state agencies. It runs in a FedRAMP High authorized environment, the federal cloud-security authorization at its High level.

Pricing is "No seat fees," with usage paid in fixed increments under a hard not-to-exceed cap. Controls include logging of administrative actions for review, two-person approval for sensitive Anthropic-side operations, and conversation history kept local on agency devices. The Claude Code command-line tool and Claude for Microsoft 365 are in early access.

On your desk: you use Claude Code daily for the Obsidian second brain, and it is now heading toward the most regulated buyers. That is a handy proof point for your Skool courses when someone asks whether this stuff is safe enough for real work. Audit logs are also a good idea to borrow for your own n8n workflows.

OpenAI launches "dots," always-on agents with their own computers

Read the brief

At DevDay 2026, announced Sept. 29, OpenAI introduced "dots." These are agents that run continuously on their own cloud computers, each with a browser and connections to 4,000+ apps, powered by GPT-6 Astra. Early reports say the first dot is included with Pro and Business Premium plans, and chats with dots do not count toward ChatGPT usage limits.

When idle, dots do read-only "proactive research," and sensitive tasks such as password changes need user approval. Pro is excluded in the EEA, Switzerland and the UK. One source so far: The Next Web covers it, and the primary OpenAI page was not opened.

On your desk: this is a store-bought version of what you hand-build in n8n and Cowork. Worth watching for the Travis Wall Studio side, such as an agent that triages student questions overnight. The approval-before-sensitive-actions design is the same rule worth putting in your own flows.

FDA hearing on psychedelics: comments due Oct. 5

This one is older than usual: the hearing happened Sept. 14, with a summary published Sept. 18. But the written comment deadline is live: Oct. 5 at 11:59 p.m. ET. The FDA held a four-hour hearing titled "Considerations for Potential Future Therapeutic Use of Psychedelic Drugs," with 80 public speakers from academia, patients, advocates, providers and industry.

A listening panel of FDA staff plus Veterans Health Administration and SAMHSA representatives heard four topics: provider training, patient safety, access, and data standards. Speaker Eddie Jacobs said the first approval is "likely to not be far off, and the conditions set at launch are likely to stick." Sourcing is thin: this comes from two secondary write-ups (UBC and TalkingDrugs), and the FDA page itself was not opened.

On your desk: for the Universe Year, this is a good look at how a regulator decides what counts as evidence for altered states. Pair it with your Hawkins, Dispenza and Jung reading, and note which claims need trial data and which are just stories.

More in Spirit

Pentagon waives NDAs for UAP whistleblowers

Read the brief

Also older than usual: DefenseScoop reported this on Sept. 14. The Pentagon issued a waiver that cancels certain non-disclosure agreements, and Special Access Program agreements (secrecy contracts for classified projects), for disclosures to representatives of the PURSUE initiative. Covered disclosures do not trigger breach penalties.

Past and present civilians, service members and contractors with UAP (unidentified anomalous phenomena, the official term for UFOs) information are directed to talk to official PURSUE representatives. Officials say the relief "establishes an authorized pipeline for systematic review, security evaluation, and potential declassification." Jordan Flowers of the Disclosure Foundation called it "a meaningful step." Confidence is medium-high on the waiver. An aggregator also mentioned a Navy release of 1,019 pages, but that was not verified, so treat it as unconfirmed.

On your desk: this is a record-keeping story, and you score remote-viewing sessions honestly. Official testimony is still testimony, so keep your usual habit: write down what is claimed, by whom, and what could confirm it.
