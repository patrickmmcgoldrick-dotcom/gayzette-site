Claude's Docs, Slides and Design leave beta and go free on every plan

AI

Anthropic offers free, opt-in security scans to open-source projects

Anthropic has opened a free service that scans open-source software for security holes, which are called vulnerabilities. Eligible projects get periodic scans from Anthropic's strongest models, and maintainers enroll by sending a pull request (a proposed code change) to anthropics/oss-scanner.

In Anthropic's own six-month trial it found over 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities. Penetration testers (people paid to break into things) checked 97 of the most serious across 48 projects; 85 (88%) were worth reporting, with one false alarm. Caveat: the reports are fully model-written with no human sorting, so some may be wrong.

On your desk: you probably will not enroll a project, but it shows how good AI code review is getting, which is the kind of help your Claude Code builds will inherit. Low priority.

Midjourney tests a Thinking Mode for more accurate pictures and text

Read the brief

Midjourney, the image-making service, is testing something it calls Thinking Mode. The goal is better prompt accuracy, typography (lettering inside images) and coherence.

It is an early test, not a launch. On alpha.midjourney.com, open a finished job's lightbox (the enlarged view) and click "Rerun (Thinking)". Midjourney may offer it more broadly later and wants feedback in Discord #ideas-and-features. Its post is a goal, not a measured result, so judge it by your own side-by-side tests rather than the promise.

On your desk: better lettering would matter for posters, thumbnails and promo art for the studio or The Stacy Show. Try rerunning one prompt both ways and compare.

Sakana's three-agent paper reviewer caught 73% of planted errors

Read the brief

Sakana AI built a reviewer from three AI agents (separate model runs that check each other) that flags where a paper's claims contradict its evidence. On the company's own test, 1,164 contradictions inserted into 257 papers, it caught 73.43% of core-claim errors; the best earlier system caught 14.81%.

It costs about $0.47 per review and needs no GPU or fine-tuning. Caveats: the test is Sakana's own, the coverage is MarkTechPost rather than the paper, and the code is "on request," not public.

On your desk: for the math-and-biology Universe Year, a cheap second reader for dense papers is a pattern you could copy.

Spirit

Paying attention to an itch may shrink the body's histamine reaction

A Nature Human Behaviour paper from Bar-Ilan University tested 37 healthy adults given a histamine skin prick (histamine is the chemical behind itching). Focusing inward on the sensation, swelling averaged 3.5 mm; distracted by a video, 5.0 mm. The red flare was 10.6 mm versus 14.0 mm.

Two follow-ups (20 and 17 people) pointed the same way, and a third experiment applied numbing cream (lidocaine) to the test site. All samples are small. The lead researcher, Rozenkrantz, calls it "an initial demonstration in a very specific experimental model, not yet something we can translate into a clinical recommendation." Coverage is PsyPost; the paper was not opened. One small study so far.

On your desk: a measured-in-millimetres example of attention doing something physical, a handy anchor for your remote-viewing practice.

More in Spirit

Virtual reality induced mystical experiences in some people, no drugs needed

Read the brief

This one is from last week (Oct 4); the beat was quiet over the weekend, so it gets a second look. A Western University study of 80 young adults (Psychology of Consciousness) asked whether virtual reality could produce a complete mystical experience.

In the guided-imagery version, 28% reported one (that is, roughly one person in four). In the guided-meditation version, 18% did. On a flat computer monitor, 0% did. Caveats: this is one small study of young adults, from self-reports, and coverage is PsyPost, not the paper.

On your desk: for remote-viewing practice, it is a reminder to note the setting you practice in, not just what you saw.

    First self-stabilizing nuclear clock built in Vienna

    Sat Oct 10

    Researchers at TU Wien built a thorium-229 nuclear clock whose laser is held steady by the atomic nuclei themselves, and it ran over 24 hours unattended (reported in Nature). Its precision is about one second of error in 30 million years, not yet as sharp as the best optical atomic clocks, with stronger lasers and better crystals planned.
