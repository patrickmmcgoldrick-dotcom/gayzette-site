Sunday · The Universe

Webb finds three feeding black holes in one young galaxy

Two of them sit 620 light-years apart and should merge; the smallest is eating faster than the usual limit.

By The Gayzette, with Claude · 2 min read · Wonder ◆◆◆◆◇

A supermassive black hole is a region where so much mass is packed so tightly that not even light escapes, and most big galaxies have one at their centre. When gas falls toward one, it heats up and glows, so astronomers call a black hole that is swallowing material "active", or feeding.

A Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics team led by Hannah Übler used NIRSpec, the James Webb Space Telescope's spectrograph (an instrument that splits light into its colours to read what made it), to study a galaxy called J0148-4214. We see it as it was 1.2 billion years after the Big Bang. Inside it they found not one feeding black hole but three, weighing about 80 million, 2 million and 600,000 times the mass of the Sun.

Two of them sit near the centre only 620 light-years apart, close by galactic standards, and the team expects them to merge within a few hundred million years. Picture three drains in one bathtub, two of them about to become one. The smallest is feeding faster than the Eddington limit, the rate at which the light pushed out by infalling gas should normally hold back more gas from falling in.

So one young galaxy shows two ways a black hole can grow at once: by merging with a neighbour, and by a small one eating harder than the textbook speed limit. The work is published in Astronomy & Astrophysics, and ScienceDaily ran the release on Oct 25.

The caveat: this is one galaxy. It shows these growth routes can happen side by side early on, not how common they were.

Suno now makes songs from inside Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini

A beta connector lets you write lyrics in a chat and get a track back, on any Suno plan, with no API key.

The caveat: this is a beta behind an early-access sign-up, so features and limits may change. And Suno is still fighting lawsuits from Universal and Sony over its training data (see Briefly).

Consciousness & The Nonphysical

The VA picks ten sites for psychedelic therapy, pending FDA approval

If a therapy is approved in early 2027, a VA official says treatment starts right away, and demand will outrun supply.

Psychedelic-assisted therapy pairs a drug such as psilocybin, the active compound in "magic mushrooms", or MDMA with sessions of psychotherapy. None is approved yet by the FDA, the US regulator that decides which medicines can be prescribed.

Read the brief

At the Psychedelic Healthcare Forum in New York, as reported Oct 22, Andrew Gradison of the Department of Veterans Affairs said the VA has selected ten initial sites, mostly ones with experience running psychedelic trials, and launched a "community of practice", a standing group where clinicians share what they learn. If therapies become available in January or February 2027, he said, the VA would begin treating patients immediately. Think of it as building the platform before the train is confirmed.

The numbers explain the planning. Of the 6.4 million veterans in regular VA care, about 2.1 million have PTSD, major depression or treatment-resistant depression, meaning depression that has not responded to standard treatment. "Demand will exceed our ability to supply these therapies," Gradison said, so the VA plans a limited rollout and will gather real-world evidence, data from everyday clinics rather than controlled trials.

FDA's Valentina Mantua and Al Bugazia of ARPA-H, a federal health research agency, also spoke on the panel.

The caveat: all of this waits on an FDA approval that has not happened, and it comes from one report of a conference panel. This is regulation news, not medical advice.

The Universe

Physicists test whether time can come from change, not a clockearly reports

A cloud of 24,000 ultracold atoms gave a lab version of an old idea about what time is.

Physics has an awkward gap. In quantum mechanics, the rules for the very small, time is an outside clock ticking in the background. In Einstein's general relativity, the rules for gravity, time is part of the stretchy fabric of the universe itself. Physicists call the mismatch "the problem of time".

Read the brief

One proposed fix says time does not need an outside clock: it can be defined by how one part of a system changes compared with another. Think of a room with no clocks, where you tell the time by how far a candle has burned down.

Giovanni Barontini's group at the University of Birmingham tested this with about 24,000 atoms cooled to a few billionths of a degree above absolute zero, the coldest anything can get. Two laser beams split the cloud into a "bright" region the researchers observed and a "dark" region they did not. Tracking how the two relate, they describe a time that emerges from the system's own internal change, which they call "entropic time" (entropy is physicists' measure of disorder).

The study is published in Physical Review Research as "Testing the problem of time with cold atoms", and ScienceDaily ran the release on Oct 30.

The caveat: this is a lab test of one model, not a verdict on what time really is, and our account comes from the institution's summary rather than the journal page.

Oxygen collisions at Brookhaven show a hint of Big Bang quark soupearly reports

A collider that ran from 2000 to 2026 made a tiny droplet of the matter that filled the first instant.

Protons and neutrons, the pieces inside every atomic nucleus, are made of smaller particles called quarks, held together by gluons (the carriers of the strong force, the glue of the nucleus). Heat matter enough and that structure melts into a quark-gluon plasma, a hot soup of free quarks and gluons, the stuff thought to have filled the universe an instant after the Big Bang.

Read the brief

The Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider at Brookhaven National Laboratory, RHIC for short, ran from 2000 to 2026, smashing atomic nuclei together to make that soup, usually with heavy gold. The STAR experiment collided much smaller oxygen nuclei at 200 GeV (a measure of collision energy), in data recorded in 2021.

How do you spot a soup that lasts less than a billionth of a billionth of a second? Indirectly. Fast particles flying out of a crash lose energy if they have to push through plasma, like a runner slowed by wading through a pool. STAR found about 20% fewer high-energy particles than expected, consistent with a tiny droplet of plasma forming. The effect is smaller than in gold collisions, as expected for a smaller system.

The result is published in Physical Review Letters, and ScienceDaily ran it on Oct 30. Europe's Large Hadron Collider ran its own first oxygen collisions in 2025.

The caveat: "consistent with" is the careful phrase here, and this account comes from one press summary rather than the paper itself.

Comedy, Puppetry & TV

YouTube tests paying some creators at $20 instead of $100

A random group of Partner Program creators in the US, India and Indonesia can cash out sooner.

When a YouTube video earns ad money, the creator is not paid view by view. Earnings pile up in AdSense, Google's payments account, and the money is only sent once the balance passes a minimum called the payout threshold. That minimum has been $100.

Read the brief

Tubefilter reported on Oct 28 that YouTube is testing a $20 threshold. It applies from October 2026 payments to a randomly selected group of creators in the YouTube Partner Program, the program that lets channels earn from ads, in the US, India and Indonesia. Creators can change their threshold in AdSense, including raising it, and the report says a wider rollout would not change Partner Program eligibility.

Think of a coffee-shop loyalty card that used to need ten stamps for a free drink and now, for some customers, needs two. A big channel crosses $100 constantly, so nothing changes for it. A small new channel earning a few dollars a month sees its first payment much sooner.

Separately, YouTube is raising the bar for Shorts pay from Feb 1, 2027 (see Briefly), so the friendlier threshold arrives alongside a stricter rule elsewhere.

The caveat: this is a test on a random group, not a rule for everyone, and the report does not say whether it will widen.

The Operator

n8n 3.0 is out; check your workflows before upgrading

The automation tool's major release adds agent-builder upgrades and removes old nodes that will now fail at run time.

n8n is a workflow automation tool: you connect boxes called nodes, each one a step such as "when an email arrives" or "add a row to a sheet", and n8n runs the chain for you. A new major version, here 3.0, is where software makers allow breaking changes, meaning some things built on the old version stop working.

Read the brief

n8n released 3.0 on Oct 27. What is new: the n8n Assistant is out of Preview, the agent builder gains a Test agent button, Slack agents can be set to reply only when someone @mentions them, and a rebuilt Edit Image node (v2) becomes the default for new workflows.

The breaking-changes page is the part to read. Removed nodes, including Function, Cron, Interval and Read PDF, fail at run time. AI Agent v1 and Edit Image v1/1.1 are gone. The Slack API credential now needs a Signing Secret, and npm/npx installs stop working, so self-hosted setups need Docker. The page says to back up the database and run Settings > Migration Report first.

Think of the Migration Report as an electrician's walk-through before a renovation: it tells you which rooms still use the old wiring before you flip the main switch.

The caveat: n8n's breaking-changes page calls 3.0 an early access release, so there is no prize for upgrading first.

The AI Builder

Resend lets AI assistants find and connect to its email service

An AI Catalog and an MCP Server Card mean a client needs only 'resend.com' to set itself up.

Resend is an email-sending service for developers. MCP, the Model Context Protocol, is a shared standard for plugging AI assistants such as Claude into outside tools, and an MCP server is the adapter on the tool's side. Connecting one has usually meant finding the right address and setup details by hand.

Read the brief

On Oct 26 Resend published two small files that do the finding for you: an AI Catalog at resend.com/.well-known/ai-catalog.json and an MCP Server Card at mcp.resend.com/mcp/server-card. The card format, SEP-2127, is an official MCP extension. Think of a business card left at the front desk: an assistant that knows only the company's name can pick it up and learn how to call.

In practice, a client that supports this discovery needs only "resend.com". You then sign in with OAuth, the familiar "log in with your account" flow, or, for setups with no person present, use an API key sent as a Bearer token, a key attached to each request. Clients without discovery can still use the hosted server at mcp.resend.com/mcp.

The bigger idea is the pattern: if more services publish cards like this, giving an assistant a new tool starts to look like typing a website name rather than reading setup docs.

The caveat: it only helps with clients that support discovery, and the changelog does not list which ones do. It was a quiet week for builder news otherwise.

Human Flourishing

Aldabra's giant tortoises number nearly 183,000, the highest count recorded

From fewer than 1,000 in the 1800s to a stable population, though older surveys likely missed many animals.

Aldabra is a remote coral atoll, a ring of coral islands around a lagoon, in the Seychelles, and it is home to giant tortoises. In the 19th century their number fell to fewer than 1,000. In 1981 the atoll became a strict reserve.

Read the brief

A University of Exeter release dated Oct 7 reports that a Seychelles Islands Foundation and Exeter team analysed five years of counts, 2018 to 2022, and put the best estimate at almost 183,000 adult and sub-adult tortoises. That is the highest figure ever recorded, and the team describes the population as stable and healthy.

Counting tortoises in scrub is harder than it sounds, because many are hidden. The team used distance sampling, a statistical method that estimates the animals you cannot see from how sightings fall off with distance from the observer. Think of counting guests at a party from the doorway, then correcting for the ones behind the furniture.

The honest caveat comes from the release itself: earlier surveys likely missed hidden tortoises, so the late-1990s figure of about 100,000 was probably an undercount and the gap to today is not all new tortoises. The real story is the long arc, from under 1,000 to a large, steady population on a protected island.

It ran in early October; it leads this desk because the week brought no fresher milestone with numbers this firm.

AI Reads the Ancients

  • BIG STORY — [PUBLISHED] Ithaca: the program that helps restore and date Greek inscriptions. Greek inscriptions, texts cut in stone, often reach us chipped, with letters missing, and without a clear date or place. In 2022 DeepMind and historians released Ithaca, a neural network (software that learns patterns from many examples) trained on a Packard Humanities Institute database of Greek inscriptions: 178,551 in the source, 78,608 in the processed training set. It suggests the missing letters and estimates where (out of 84 regions) and when a text was made. In the Nature paper its top guess filled gaps correctly 61.8% of the time, it named the right region 70.8% of the time, and its dates landed a median of 3 years from the accepted range (mean about 29 years). Two ancient-history graduate students restored about 50 inscriptions with and without it; DeepMind reports their accuracy rose from 25% alone to 72% with Ithaca. On disputed Athenian decrees long dated to about 446/445 BC, its estimates landed about 5 years from newer scholarly proposals in the 420s BC, against about 27 years for the old labels. Ithaca does not read an unknown script; the Greek was always readable. It offers ranked guesses, and historians check them and make the call. It is a 2022 result, run as part of our catch-up on how AI came to the ancients. On your desk: the Library OS shelf, as an early, well-measured case of 'the machine suggests, the expert decides'. The Droplet Desk

  • NEW THIS WEEK — THE SCIENCE, PLAIN: [PUBLISHED] Spider silk starts as droplets. Before it is a thread, spider silk is 'dope', a liquid of proteins, water and salt stored in the spider's gland. Gregory Holland's lab at San Diego State University, with Hannah Johnson and Kevin Chalek, used NMR (a method that reads how atoms are arranged using magnets and radio waves) on black-widow silk dope. When phosphate is added, the proteins gather into condensates, semi-liquid droplets with no membrane around them, like oil beading in vinaigrette. Two amino acids, arginine and tyrosine, act as weak 'molecular stickers' that pull proteins together and squeeze water out, and the same contacts are still there in the finished solid fiber, so the droplet stage seems to guide the final structure. Limits: lab-made silk is still weaker than the spider's, and the full set of triggers is not known. The paper is in PNAS; it runs now because Quanta's Oct 28 feature gave it fresh coverage and no newer droplet paper turned up this week. WHERE IT TOUCHES OUR WORK (RESONANCE): water pushed out as order forms echoes the Buffalo RNA-droplet finding, where RNA keeps fewer water molecules around it as it comes together: a different molecule, the same pattern. That is our parallel, not the authors' claim. (RESONANCE) A liquid stage before a solid structure rhymes with De Stefano's idea of the first boundary; the study says nothing about consciousness or meaning. Outlook from the CPC discussion dated 2026-10-08.

Tropical cyclone: The umbrella word for a rotating storm over warm ocean water: tropical depression, tropical storm or hurricane. · El Nino: A warming of the surface water in the central and eastern tropical Pacific that shifts weather patterns worldwide, often making the US West wetter in winter. · ENSO: El Nino-Southern Oscillation: the swing between warm (El Nino), cool (La Nina) and in-between (ENSO-neutral) ocean conditions in the tropical Pacific. · El Nino Advisory: NOAA's formal notice that El Nino conditions are present and expected to continue. · Nino-3.4 index: The ocean temperature, compared with normal, in a patch of the central Pacific that forecasters use to rate El Nino. +2.1C means 2.1 degrees Celsius (about 3.8 F) warmer than average. · RONI: Relative Oceanic Nino Index: a version of the Nino index adjusted for overall ocean warming, used to rank how strong an El Nino is. · Strong-to-very-strong event: A big El Nino, meaning the Pacific stays well above average temperature for several months.
