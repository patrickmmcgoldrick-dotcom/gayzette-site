FDA approves the first heart valve that grows with the child

The Autus valve goes in at 13 mm and can later be widened to 22 mm with a balloon, which could spare kids repeat open-heart surgeries.

By The Gayzette, with Claude · 2 min read · Wonder ◆◆◆◆◇

Think about buying shoes for a growing kid. The feet get bigger, so the shoes get replaced, again and again. Now picture the shoe as a valve inside the heart, where every new size has meant another open-heart operation. That has been the bind for children who need a new pulmonary valve, the one-way door that lets blood leave the heart for the lungs.

On Oct 1 the FDA approved the Autus Size-Adjustable Valve, the first heart valve designed to grow with the child. Surgeons implant it at 13 millimeters. Later, as the child grows, a doctor can guide a balloon catheter (a thin tube with a small balloon on its tip, threaded in through a blood vessel) to the valve and inflate it, widening the valve to as much as 22 millimeters. The FDA says this can reduce repeat open-heart surgeries. It is also the first FDA-approved valve whose leaflets, the little flaps that open and close with each beat, are made of a synthetic polymer instead of animal tissue.

The receipts come from a study of 62 children at 12 U.S. sites. At six months, all 60 patients who were evaluated had acceptable blood flow through the valve, with no more than mild leakage.

The caveat: six months is a short look at a device meant to last through years of growth and more than one widening, so the long-term record is still to be written. Even so, it is a lovely bit of engineering, a part designed from day one for a body that will not stay the same size.

Also leading

NASA's Roman telescope can hold steady on a dime 150 miles away

Fine-guidance tests wrapped Sept 21, and the coronagraph, the part built to blot out starlight, saw first light.

A telescope is only as good as its grip. Take a photo with a shaky hand and the picture smears. Point a telescope that drifts by a hair at a faint galaxy and you get the same smear, just billions of miles wide. So before a space telescope does any science, engineers test how still it can hold.

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope just passed those tests, called fine guidance, between Sept 15 and 21. NASA's comparison: its pointing is steady enough to aim a laser at a dime about 150 miles away, and more tuning is expected to stretch that to about 230 miles. Fine guidance works a bit like keeping a camera level by watching the horizon: the telescope locks onto reference stars and makes tiny, constant corrections.

The second milestone is the Coronagraph Instrument. A coronagraph is a mask that blocks a star's glare so fainter things right next to it, like planets, can show up, the same trick as holding your thumb over the sun to spot a bird beside it. On Sept 22 and 27 it got first light, imaging stars in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small neighbor galaxy, and confirming it can make a focused image.

The caveat: these are engineering checks, not discoveries. First light means the instrument works; it has not imaged a planet yet. That work comes later. This week was about proving the hands are steady, and they are.

Consciousness & The Nonphysical

A VR headset gave some people full mystical experiences, no drugs

In a Western University study, 28% of a VR guided-imagery group reported a complete mystical experience. On flat screens: none.

Psychedelic researchers have a yardstick for the big stuff: questionnaires that score whether a person had a "mystical experience," and how fully. A "complete" score means clearing the high bar on that scale, not just feeling relaxed. It is how a lab turns something people struggle to describe into a number it can compare.

Read the brief

A team at Western University (Miller, Nyland and Frewen) ran 80 young adults through guided imagery or meditation, delivered either on an immersive VR headset or an ordinary flat monitor. In VR, 28% of the guided-imagery group and 18% of the meditation group reported complete mystical experiences. On flat screens, nobody did. VR also raised awe, serenity and presence, the sense of actually being somewhere. Fantastical scenes and realistic ones worked about equally well.

The interesting part is that the container seems to matter. The same meditation, wrapped around you instead of sitting in a rectangle on a desk, produced the kind of experience the psychedelic literature usually studies with drugs. The study appears in Psychology of Consciousness: Theory, Research, and Practice, and PsyPost covered it on Oct 4.

The caveats: 80 people is a small group, young adults are one slice of humanity, and the experiences are self-reported. We read PsyPost's summary rather than the paper itself. One study so far, and a fun one.

The Universe

Half your genome is jumping genes, and they helped build us

Quanta traces how mobile DNA, often filed under "junk," is tied to eyes, immune systems, placentas and one famous moth.

Picture a cookbook in which some recipes can photocopy themselves and paste the copy onto a random page. Mostly that is clutter. Once in a while it produces a dish nobody would have invented on purpose. That is roughly what transposons are: stretches of DNA that copy themselves and move around a genome. Nearly half the human genome is made of them.

Read the brief

They have often been lumped in with "junk DNA." A Quanta Magazine piece by Jake Buehler lays out how that picture has turned around: transposons are now linked to the evolution of eyes, immune systems and placentas. The thread starts with Barbara McClintock's 1944 work on maize, where she first saw genes moving, and runs through today's genome sequencing.

The single best example is the peppered moth, a biology-class favorite. Its dark-winged form traces back to a transposon that landed in a gene called cortex, an insertion dated to 1819. One jump, in one gene, and a moth changed color.

The caveat lives in the word "partners." A self-copying stretch of DNA is not trying to help anyone. The useful insertions are the ones that stuck around because they worked, which is how evolution keeps score. Note too that this piece is from Sept 21, a little older than most of this week's news, and it earns its place because the cells-and-DNA side of the beat was quiet.

it is core cells-and-DNA material for the Universe Year, and a ready answer the next time someone says most of our DNA is junk.

A 55-year-old math puzzle is cracked, for large primes, by randomness

Ronald Graham asked in 1971 whether numbers can always be ordered so no two running totals land on the same spot. Four papers now prove it for sets of every size, though only for very large primes.

Here is the puzzle in clock terms. Take a clock with a prime number of hours, say 7. Pick some of the hours, but not the zero. Can you always put your picks in an order so that, as you add them one by one and move around the clock, every running total lands on a different spot? Mathematicians call this working "mod p" (clock arithmetic with p hours), and Ronald Graham asked whether the answer is always yes in 1971.

Read the brief

It took 55 years and four papers. Müyesser and Pokrovskiy closed one piece, Kravitz and Bedert another, and Sauermann and Pham closed the remaining gap in a 27-page paper in February 2026. Quanta wrote up the whole arc on Sept 28.

The trick is called the probabilistic method. Instead of building the perfect order by hand, you show that a random shuffle works often enough that a good order must exist. It is like proving a haystack holds a needle by showing that a random handful has a decent chance of containing one. The tools were anti-concentration (showing the running totals do not pile up on a few spots) and Fourier analysis, which breaks a pattern into waves the way your ear splits a chord into notes.

The caveat: the proof works only for very large primes (think 10 to the 100th power and up), so the conjecture is technically still open for smaller p; and it says a good order exists; it does not hand you a quick recipe for finding one. And the news hook is the write-up, since the final paper came out in February.

it is beginner-friendly math for the Universe Year, a question you can state over dinner, solved with the "random works often enough" trick, and a different flavor from the graph-sandwich proof that ran earlier.

The Dance World

Kyle Abraham's company pays dancers year-round and shares royaltiesearly reports

Renamed Kyle Abraham Dance Vanguard, the company offers 52-week salaries, retirement benefits and perpetual royalties on new work.

Every dance company has to answer two money questions. What do dancers earn in the weeks with no shows? And when a piece a dancer helped build is performed again years later, who gets paid?

Read the brief

Kyle Abraham's company has a new answer, and a new name. Founded in 2005 as Abraham.In.Motion and renamed A.I.M in 2019, it now operates as Kyle Abraham Dance Vanguard, its third name. Per Dance Magazine on Sept 28, dancers get 52-week salaries, retirement benefits and perpetual royalties on new works they contribute to. A royalty is a cut each time the work earns in the future, the way a songwriter gets paid when a song is played again, and "perpetual" means it does not expire.

Abraham says the name nods partly to D'Angelo and his band, the Vanguard. The company also plans to move beyond the stage into dance films, installations and merchandise, which gives the work more places to live, and more ways to earn.

The caveat: this is one report, and it does not spell out salary levels or how the royalty split works, which are the details that matter most to anyone thinking of copying the model. Still, a choreographer-led company putting dancers on year-round pay with a stake in the work is a real shift in how the business can be built.

The Operator

Stripe teaches shopping bots to handle surprise prices and bank checks

Link now lets AI agents ask for a higher approved amount when tax or shipping changes the total, and walks them through 3D Secure.

An "agentic purchase" is when an AI assistant buys something on a person's behalf. Two ordinary things trip those bots up. The price can change after the card is approved, once tax or shipping is added. And the bank can step in with 3D Secure, the "confirm it's really you" check that pops up during some online payments.

Read the brief

On Sept 29 Stripe said Link, its saved-payment wallet, now handles both. It supports incremental authorization, which lets an agent ask for a higher approved amount when the final price changes, the way a hotel adjusts the hold on your card at checkout. Agents also get guidance such as a 3D Secure verification URL, so the bot knows where to send the human for the bank's check. And shoppers get free purchase protections covering accidental damage, lost items, price drops and returns.

The headline number: Stripe reports agentic purchases made with Link rose 38 times over the past month. That figure is Stripe's own.

The caveat: nobody is booking a dance class through a bot tomorrow, and 38x is a growth multiple, not a size. What it shows is the plumbing being laid. When customers start arriving through assistants, the payment rails will already know how to take them.

On our desk

Stripe is the payments layer under the studio's Acuity booking, so agent-initiated checkout is the next kind of customer that flow may meet, and it is worth knowing the rails exist before the first one shows up.

The AI Builder

Claude Sonnet 5.5: same price, faster, and cheaper per job

Anthropic kept the $2/$10 per-million-token price and says output is 30%+ faster, with cost per task down by up to 30%.

First, how the meter runs. AI models are billed by the token, a chunk of text roughly the size of a short word. You pay one rate for what you send in and a higher rate for what comes back. Sonnet 5.5 costs $2 per million tokens in and $10 per million out, with cache reads (text the model already read and can reuse, like a bookmark) at $0.20 per million.

Read the brief

Anthropic released Sonnet 5.5 on Sept 28 at exactly the same prices as before. The difference is in the work: Anthropic says output comes 30% or more faster than Sonnet 5, and cost per task drops by up to 30% because the model uses fewer tokens and fewer tool calls to finish the same job. Same price per gallon, better mileage.

On benchmarks, Anthropic lists 70.6% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, a test of tasks done at the command line, and 80.1% (a partial score) on OSWorld 2.1, which measures computer use, the model operating a desktop the way a person would.

The caveat: the speed and savings figures are Anthropic's own, and "up to" describes the best case. Real savings depend on the job, so the honest test is to run your own flows on it and compare the bill.

Human Flourishing

Five places in the Americas recognized for ending old diseases

PAHO honored Brazil, the Bahamas, Turks and Caicos, El Salvador and Chile for stopping mother-to-baby HIV, trachoma, leprosy and more.

In public health, "elimination" has a precise meaning. It does not mean a disease has vanished from the planet. It means a country has stopped it working as a public-health problem, or shut down one particular route of spread, and an outside health body has checked the numbers and signed off. Think of it less as a trophy and more as a passed inspection.

Read the brief

On Sept 29, at PAHO's Directing Council in Washington, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO, the World Health Organization's office for the Americas) recognized five countries and territories whose achievements were certified, validated or verified by WHO between December 2025 and September 2026. Brazil and the Bahamas ended mother-to-child transmission of HIV, meaning babies are no longer being infected by their mothers in pregnancy or birth at a public-health scale. Turks and Caicos did the same for both HIV and hepatitis B. El Salvador eliminated trachoma, an infectious eye disease. Chile was recognized for leprosy and for rabies carried by dogs.

The bigger frame: the region has set itself a goal of eliminating more than 30 communicable diseases by 2030, and about 60% of the countries worldwide recognized for ending mother-to-child HIV transmission are in the Americas. Every one of these recognitions sits on years of unglamorous work: testing, treatment and record-keeping.

One honest note: Chile's rabies validation already ran in this paper in mid-September, so the new news here is the regional ceremony and the other four. And elimination is a status a country has to keep, not a finish line it crosses once.

The Claim Ledger: Was Everything He Said a Lie?

  • CLAIM: At an April 2025 HHS/FDA briefing, Kennedy announced a phase-out of petroleum-based food dyes, and the briefing tied them to children's health problems. (The long list of conditions read out at that briefing, from ADHD to cancer, was voiced by then-FDA Commissioner Makary, who listed ADHD, obesity, diabetes, cancer and gastrointestinal issues; Kennedy's own remarks stressed industry cooperation.) — GRADE: PARTLY. Why: California's OEHHA concluded in 2021 that synthetic dyes are associated with hyperactivity and other behavioral effects in some children, and that older federal safety thresholds were not designed to detect this; that is the strongest support. Links to cancer, obesity or diabetes in people are not established in the cited assessment. The federal phase-out relies on voluntary industry action, not a ban. (Also: https://abcnews.com/US/rfk-jr-plans-phase-artificial-food-dyes-us/story?id=121034287) California OEHHA, 16 Apr 2021: synthetic food dyes assessment
  • CLAIM: The May 2025 MAHA Commission report on childhood chronic disease, released under Kennedy, names a poor diet heavy in ultra-processed foods first among four drivers, and Kennedy called it a clarion call to act with utmost urgency. — GRADE: PARTLY (on the underlying science; the report does say this). Why: a Lancet series (Nov 2025) finds ultra-processed food associated with higher risk of multiple chronic diseases. But outside statisticians note most of the evidence is observational, meaning it follows what people eat rather than testing a change, so it cannot prove cause, and randomized trials are limited. How much is the processing itself, versus sugar, salt, calories or overall diet, is unsettled. (Also: https://www.cbsnews.com/news/rfk-jr-maha-report-childhood-chronic-disease/) Science Media Centre, 18 Nov 2025: expert reaction to the Lancet UPF series
  • CLAIM: The same report says over 40% of US children have at least one chronic condition and that childhood obesity now exceeds 20%. — GRADE: VERIFIED for the obesity level; PARTLY for the wider chronic-disease figure. Why: CDC's national survey, which measures height and weight, put obesity among US children and teens aged 2 to 19 at 21.1% in August 2021 to August 2023, up from 19.3% in 2017 to 2018. The 40% figure depends on how "chronic condition" is defined, and CBS reported that federal health officials said the report left out documented risk factors such as maternal health issues and preterm birth. (Also: https://www.cbsnews.com/news/rfk-jr-maha-report-childhood-chronic-disease/) American Hospital Association, 26 Feb 2026: CDC obesity figures
  • CLAIM: In April 2025, on the CDC's new autism figures, Kennedy said autism is preventable, that it is known to come from an environmental exposure, and that genes do not cause epidemics. — GRADE: UNSUPPORTED. Why: the measured figure did rise, to 1 in 31 eight-year-olds (2022 data). But CDC and researchers attribute much of the rise to broader diagnostic criteria (changed in 1994 and 2013), more screening and more awareness; no environmental factor has been shown to explain it; and heritability studies find strong genetic contributions. His side's strongest point: diagnostic change may not account for all of the increase, and environmental factors are under-researched. Saying it is known goes beyond the evidence. PBS NewsHour, Apr 2025: fact-checking the environmental-autism claim
  • CLAIM: Kennedy has said Americans are being "unknowingly poisoned" by seed oils and has touted beef tallow (X post). — GRADE: UNSUPPORTED. Why: an observational study of 221,054 adults, summarized by the American Heart Association, linked the highest plant-oil intake to a 16% lower risk of premature death. Breakdown products from heated oils show harm in cells and animals, but no direct human evidence was cited. Fair to his side: observational data and older trials are imperfect. But "poison" is not supported by human evidence. (Also: https://www.yahoo.com/lifestyle/rfk-jr-says-americans-being-162720070.html ; https://extension.unh.edu/blog/2025/08/are-seed-oils-toxic) American Heart Association newsroom: plant-based oils and premature death
  • CLAIM: In September 2025 HHS and FDA began a label change for acetaminophen (Tylenol) in pregnancy; the press release cited a potential association with autism, contrary studies showing none, and a lack of clear causal evidence. Kennedy later said the evidence was not enough to say it definitely causes autism but called it very suggestive. — GRADE: UNSUPPORTED as a causal claim; the "association" wording in the release was accurate. Why: a January 2026 systematic review of 43 studies in a Lancet journal found no evidence that acetaminophen in pregnancy raises the risk of autism, ADHD or intellectual disability. Sibling-comparison studies, which compare brothers and sisters to cancel out shared genes and home life, show no effect, and earlier small associations are probably explained by the mother's fever, pain or genetics. His side's strongest version: earlier meta-analyses did show small associations, and he hedged; the sibling evidence undercuts it. (Also: https://www.staradvertiser.com/2025/10/29/breaking-news/rfk-jr-says-data-does-not-show-tylenol-causes-autism/) EurekAlert (Lancet journal release), 16 Jan 2026: prenatal paracetamol review
  • CLAIM: Kennedy called the supplementary data of a large July 2025 Danish study of aluminum-containing vaccines "calamitous evidence of harm," cited from it a 67% higher risk of Asperger's syndrome per milligram of aluminum exposure, and asked the journal to retract this "badly flawed study". — GRADE: UNSUPPORTED (the 67% figure is a real result, but selectively used; the broader vaccine-autism link he has argued is also unsupported). Why: the study followed more than 1.2 million children and found no link between aluminum-containing vaccines and autism or other chronic conditions. The 67% figure is one result from supplementary analyses involving 540 comparisons, where some chance findings are expected, and the journal's editor defended the paper. His fair point is that registry studies have limits; this particular number rests on 51 children and a wide confidence interval (1% to 177%), and the authors said the supplementary analyses were not suitable for causal interpretation. The grade is on this claim, not on every vaccine-safety question (HHS-run reviews are ongoing). FactCheck.org, Sep 2025: aluminum-containing vaccines
  • CLAIM: In July 2025, approving the removal of thimerosal (a mercury-containing preservative) from US flu vaccines, Kennedy said in the HHS release injecting any amount of mercury into children when mercury-free alternatives exist defies common sense. — GRADE: UNSUPPORTED as a claim of harm. Why: STAT reports overwhelming evidence that the amount of thimerosal in vaccines poses no health risk. Thimerosal was already out of routine US childhood vaccines, so the practical change was limited to multi-dose flu vials. Choosing caution when an alternative exists is a defensible policy view; the evidence does not back the harm claim. (Also: https://www.statnews.com/2025/07/23/kennedy-approves-acip-recommendation-thimerosal-removed-from-flu-vaccines/) HHS, 23 Jul 2025: thimerosal removed from flu vaccines
  • CLAIM: In November 2024 Kennedy wrote that fluoride is an industrial waste associated with arthritis, bone fractures, bone cancer, IQ loss, neurodevelopmental disorders and thyroid disease. — GRADE: PARTLY. Why: the National Toxicology Program concluded in August 2024, with moderate confidence, that fluoride above 1.5 mg/L is associated with lower IQ in children, but found too little data on the 0.7 mg/L level used in US water. Only the IQ association is supported by the cited assessment, and only above 1.5 mg/L; harm at US water levels is not established either way. (Also: https://www.cnn.com/2024/11/03/health/rfk-jr-fluoride-science/index.html) National Toxicology Program, 21 Aug 2024: fluoride assessment
  • CLAIM: A recurring theme of his writing and campaigns: regulators are too tied to the industries they oversee. In a late-2024 X post, made before the November election, he told FDA staff he described as part of a corrupt system to preserve their records and pack their bags, and MAHA calls for ending the revolving door between industry and government. — GRADE: VERIFIED that the financial ties exist; PARTLY, and contested, on whether they bias decisions. Why: per Forbes, user fees from drug and device makers were $3.3 billion of FDA's $7.2 billion budget in 2024, about 46%, under the 1992 PDUFA law. That is on the record. Whether the fees bend reviews is debated: they were designed to speed reviews, defenders say decisions are firewalled, and evidence that fees change outcomes is mixed. We found no direct evidence either way on calling staff corrupt. Forbes, 4 Dec 2024: Kennedy on industry links to the FDA

The Take (the paper's own view, labelled as opinion)

  • What is working. Opinion: the movement's strongest ground is the food system, and there the record has substance. Our ledger grades its worry about synthetic dyes and about ultra-processed food as partly right, and the rise in childhood obesity as verified. The actions that followed are concrete and checkable: a proposed end to companies self-certifying additives without telling FDA, dyes gone from General Mills' US cereals, a $1B farm program, FDA letters and data put in public view. A critic and a supporter can both go and look. HHS, 12 Aug 2026: GRAS proposal and food-policy reforms
  • What is not. Where Kennedy has spoken with the most certainty, the evidence we graded does not back him: that autism's rise is known to be environmental, that seed oils poison people, that acetaminophen in pregnancy causes autism, that thimerosal harms children, and the 67% aluminum figure, a real number taken from supplementary analyses the study's authors cautioned against reading causally. The pattern in the ledger is that a sound concern and an unsupported certainty often arrive in the same sentence. That matters most where the stakes are personal, in vaccines and pregnancy. EurekAlert (Lancet journal release), 16 Jan 2026: prenatal paracetamol review
  • What to watch. Three markers will show whether the food agenda becomes rules or stays pledges: whether the GRAS notification rule is finalized, whether the ultra-processed food definition clears White House review and is published, and how the Texas warning-label case fares before its 1 Jan 2027 date. Because the dye phase-out is voluntary, the industry deadlines running through end of 2027 are the real test. Food Safety Magazine, 10 Aug 2026: UPF definition sent for final review
  • Holding both at once. A reader who admired Kennedy can hold all of this without contradiction: he pointed at real problems (dyes in some children, diets heavy in ultra-processed food, rising childhood obesity, an FDA funded heavily by the companies it regulates), and on several of his most confident claims the evidence does not agree with him. Was everything he said a lie? The ledger does not support that reading, and it does not support the opposite either: two claims verified in part, three partly right, five unsupported. "Lie" is also a word about intent, which grades cannot measure. The fairer weekly question is narrower: is this claim supported, and is this policy being carried out? Forbes, 4 Dec 2024: Kennedy on industry links to the FDA

Behind the paper

How this paper is made

A newsroom has a lot of jobs and only a few of them need a big brain. The trick is to give each job the cheapest model that can do it well, and to keep the one that writes and the one that checks separate so the paper never grades its own homework. Here is exactly what ran to make this issue, with the token counts. A token is a chunk of text about three quarters of a word; it's the unit these models are metered in.
