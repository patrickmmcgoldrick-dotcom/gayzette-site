Sunday · The Universe

Webb finds a record-distance lensing galaxy cluster

XLSSC 122 sits about 10.4 billion light-years away, and its mass is packed toward the middle more tightly than the standard cosmological model expects.

The Gayzette · Wonder ◆◆◆◆◇

Mass bends light. Pile enough of it in one place, such as a galaxy cluster (a big family of galaxies held together by gravity), and it behaves like the thick glass base of a wine glass: light from galaxies far behind it gets stretched and smeared into bright arcs. Astronomers call this strong gravitational lensing, and the arcs are useful, because their shape tells you how the mass in front is laid out.

The James Webb Space Telescope has now captured the first lensing arcs around a cluster called XLSSC 122, about 10.4 billion light-years away. That makes it the most distant cluster known to show strong lensing. The results are in three papers in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, with Kyle Finner of Caltech's IPAC lead author of the first.

The surprise is the layout. The cluster's mass is unusually concentrated near its center, and that conflicts with what the standard cosmological model, the working recipe for how the universe grew its structures, predicts for a cluster at that early epoch. Webb also made the earliest known detection of intracluster light: the faint, diffuse starlight from stars drifting between the cluster's galaxies rather than living inside any one of them.

Think of it as finding a city that, at a very young age, already has a dense downtown the planners said should take much longer to build.

The caveat: this is one cluster. A single object that disagrees with a model is a question for the model, not a verdict on it, and the useful next step is checking whether other distant clusters look the same.

Also leading

Piping plovers hit a record 99 pairs on the Great Lakes

From about a dozen pairs in 1990 to 99 nesting pairs and 184 fledged chicks in 2026, the fifth record year in a row.

The piping plover is a small, endangered shorebird, and the Great Lakes population came close to vanishing. By 1990 it was down to about a dozen pairs, all of them in northern Michigan.

This year, Audubon Great Lakes reports, a record 99 pairs nested around the lakes, up from 88 in 2025. A record 184 chicks fledged (160 wild-raised plus 24 captive-reared), meaning they grew up enough to fly. It is the fifth straight record year, so this is a trend, not a lucky summer.

One detail is pleasingly specific: plovers nested on Cat Island in Green Bay for the first time in 75 years. A bird coming back to a spot it last used in the 1950s is what recovery looks like at the scale of a single island.

Do the arithmetic and the curve is the point. About 12 pairs to 99 is roughly an eightfold rise in 36 years. It is slow, unglamorous and counted one nest at a time, which is how most real turnarounds happen.

The honest frame: 99 pairs is still a small population, and Audubon's own headline says the records bring the birds closer to recovery, not that they have recovered.

How the first FDA psychedelic approval is being set up

A trade newsletter lays out the plumbing: Compass's expected filing, a Spravato-style safety program, an MDMA refiling and $4.7M for California veteran research.

Approval is only the front door. Before a psychedelic can be prescribed in the US, someone has to settle who may give it, where, and how patients are watched. That machinery has a name: a REMS, short for Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy, the FDA's term for a required safety program attached to a drug.

Psychedelic Alpha's practitioner newsletter (Oct 14) reports that Compass Pathways, which is developing a psilocybin treatment called COMP360, expects to file its new drug application this quarter, with a decision in up to 4 months and a launch in the first half of 2027. Compass expects a REMS like Spravato's, with at least 6 hours of monitoring. The newsletter also says Compass based its payer talks on Spravato's annualised price, around $50,000 to $65,000; that is a benchmark, not a COMP360 price.

Two other pieces moved. Resilient, the company formerly called Lykos, has, the newsletter understands, refiled its application for MDMA to treat PTSD without completing a fresh Phase III trial in-house (the large final stage of testing), leaning on other groups' studies; neither Resilient nor the FDA has commented. And California has budgeted $4.7 million for psychedelic research for veterans. Next on the regulatory calendar: the FDA's request for information on ibogaine closes for comments on Nov 20.

The caveat: this is one trade newsletter, and the Compass timeline is the newsletter's reporting of company statements, not an FDA schedule. It is also regulation and logistics only. Nothing here is a claim about what these drugs do for any one person.

The Universe

Black holes of wildly different sizes launch jets at one feeding rate

Stars shredded by black holes show delayed radio jets switching on at about 2% of the Eddington limit, the same threshold seen in small Milky Way black holes.

When a star wanders too close to a black hole, gravity pulls harder on its near side than its far side, and the star is stretched apart. Astronomers call this a tidal disruption event. The black hole then feeds on the wreckage, and sometimes, after a delay, it launches a jet: a narrow, fast beam of material that shows up in radio telescopes.

To compare how fast different black holes eat, astronomers use the Eddington limit, roughly the fastest a black hole can feed before the light from its own meal starts pushing the food away. It works like a speed limit that scales with the black hole's size, so feeding rates can be compared as a percentage of it.

Goodwin and Mummery, in a Nature Astronomy paper announced by Curtin University, studied 10 usable tidal disruption events. The delayed radio jets appeared when feeding fell to about 2% of the Eddington limit. The same roughly 2% threshold had already been tied to jets from small black holes in our own Milky Way. So objects that differ in mass by millions of times appear to follow one rule.

Picture a garden tap and a fire hydrant that both start to sputter at the same fraction of full flow. When a single number holds across a size gap that large, it hints that the switch belongs to the physics of feeding itself rather than to any one kind of black hole.

The caveat: 10 events is a small sample, so this is a strong pattern so far, not a closed case.

The Universe

Webb reads the debris of Mars-sized worlds smashing together

Of 21 extreme debris disks around young stars, about a third are silica-rich, a sign of violent collisions between Mars-sized bodies.

Young planetary systems are rough neighborhoods. Rocky bodies collide, and the collisions leave dust. Around a few young stars that dust is thick enough to earn a name: an extreme debris disk, a bright ring of fresh rubble.

Kate Su of the Space Science Institute and colleagues studied 21 of these disks, 5 observed by the Spitzer telescope and 16 by Webb, in The Astrophysical Journal (published Oct 1). They read the dust's chemistry like a crash report. About one-third of the disks are silica-rich, which points to violent collisions between Mars-sized bodies. The other two-thirds are silica-poor, which points to lower-energy impacts, including grazing impacts between Moon-sized bodies. Silica is the mineral family behind sand and glass, so its abundance works as a clue to how hard the hit was.

This stage is rare. Only about 1% of young stars show it, which is why a sample of 21 is worth having. And it is close to home: the work relates to the giant impact that made our own Moon, so these disks are a look at the kind of crash that shaped the sky we see every night.

The caveat: these are snapshots of 21 systems, not a movie of one, and the release is 11 days old, at the outer edge of this week's window.

The Dance World

Patricia Delgado's first ballet as a choreographer premieres with a live singer

Miami City Ballet opened Push the Buttons of the Civilized on Oct 16, with Emily Estefan performing live onstage.

Patricia Delgado danced for 17 years with Miami City Ballet. Now she is making the work instead of only performing it. On Oct 16 the company premiered Push the Buttons of the Civilized in its American Rhythms program, the first ballet she has created on her own, one of three works on the program.

The music is the unusual part. Singer-songwriter Emily Estefan performs live onstage, with most of the music drawn from her 2017 debut album, on vocals, guitar and drums, while the orchestra in the pit plays her songs. Picture a concert and a ballet sharing one stage, with the artist standing inside the dance rather than hidden away below it.

Delgado told Pointe what the new role feels like: "This feels like I have the ability to craft the dance in any way I want." For a dancer who spent nearly two decades executing other people's choices, that sentence is the whole career turn in miniature.

Per Miami City Ballet's page, the program runs Oct 16-25: Miami's Arsht Center through Oct 18, then the Broward Center in Fort Lauderdale on Oct 24-25.

The caveat: Pointe's piece ran on Oct 8, before opening night, so this is a preview of what she set out to make, not a review of how it landed.

The Dance World

Olympic ice dancer Tessa Virtue makes her professional dance debut

The two-time Olympic ice-dance champion performs a solo premiere by Alyssa Martin at Fall for Dance North in Toronto.

Ice dance and concert dance share a vocabulary of line, musicality and partnering. The difference is that one happens on blades under judges, and the other happens on a stage where the only score is the audience's attention.

Tessa Virtue, a two-time Olympic ice-dance champion, is crossing that line. She performs solo in The Unruly Heart, a premiere by choreographer Alyssa Martin, the festival's artist in residence, at Fall for Dance North in Toronto. Rehearsals began in July. National Ballet of Canada principal Heather Ogden brought the three of them together, and Scott Moir, Virtue's skating partner of 22 years, is in the audience this time rather than beside her.

There is a road not taken in the story: at 9, Virtue turned down a spot at Canada's National Ballet School to pursue ice dancing. Her pro dance debut is that other door, opened years later.

The festival, now in its 12th edition, runs Oct 15-25. Martin's piece sat on the LEGENDS program, Oct 15-17.

The caveat: Dance Magazine wrote this up ahead of the performances, so we can say what the piece is and how it came together, not how it went.

Books & Publishing

Authors' groups across three continents publish a charter for ethical AI use

Signers pledge to publish an AI-use policy, back consent-based systems, and disclose AI content that forms a material part of a work.

Generative AI, software that produces text or images from a prompt, raises two plain questions for anyone who writes for a living: who gets asked, and who gets told? A coalition of writers' organizations has put its answer on one page.

The UK Society of Authors, the Creators' Rights Alliance, the Authors Guild in the US, and the Australian, Canadian and New Zealand author bodies released a shared charter for the ethical use of generative AI, built on UNESCO's 10 principles for responsible AI. The Society of Authors' release is dated Oct 14; Publishing Perspectives reported it the next day.

Among the commitments, signatories pledge three things that matter most here. They will publish a policy saying what AI use they permit and how they disclose it. They will back consent-based systems that include the rightsholder's permission. And they will disclose AI-generated content when it forms a material part of a work. That last phrase carries the weight: a spell-checker is not a co-author, but a chapter drafted by a model may well be a material part.

Early backers named include Kazuo Ishiguro, Tracy Chevalier and Kester Brewin, and anyone can sign at ethicalaicharter.com.

The caveat: the article gives no signatory count, so it is too early to say how widely the charter has caught on.

The Operator

The SBA gives content creators a federal business code: NAICS 5121

The agency's first guidance naming creators files them under Motion Picture and Video Industries for SBA lending and contracting.

A NAICS code is the number the US government uses to sort businesses by what they do, a bit like a library call number for a company. Lenders and federal programs read it to decide which rules and size limits apply to you.

On Oct 15 the US Small Business Administration issued its first guidance that names content creators, placing them under NAICS 5121, Motion Picture and Video Industries, for SBA lending and contracting. Net Influencer reports that this covers 27 million creator-led businesses.

Creators were already eligible for SBA programs. What they lacked was a clear answer about which code applied, so two businesses that both sell video could reasonably have filled in different boxes. The SBA is also weighing a permanent creator-specific exception, and a comment period on the SBA's broader size-standards rule (the cutoffs for what counts as a small business) closes on Nov 20.

Think of it as the government finally printing a label for a drawer people were already using.

The caveat: guidance names a code; it does not hand out money. It makes the forms clearer, not the approvals easier.

The AI Builder

Claude now works inside Google Docs, Sheets and Slides

A sidebar in public beta on all paid plans reads your file and selection, edits in place, and asks before each change by default.

Until now, using Claude on a document meant a round trip: copy the text out, paste it into a chat, copy the answer back. Anthropic has moved Claude into the document. A Claude sidebar now runs inside Google Docs, Sheets and Slides; you install it from the Google Workspace Marketplace, then open it from Extensions > Claude.

It is in public beta on all paid plans. Claude can read the file and whatever you have selected, and edit it directly. In Sheets it writes formulas, pivot tables (summary tables that regroup rows by category) and native charts. In Slides it builds from the deck's existing layouts and checks for overlapping elements.

The default mode is "Ask before edits": each proposed change arrives as an approval card you accept or reject, a bit like tracked changes from a collaborator. Separately, Google Docs, Sheets and Slides connectors let you create and edit those files from inside a Claude chat.

The caveat: it is a beta, Anthropic's page says nothing about free plans, and we have not tested it ourselves, so treat the feature list as the vendor's own description until it has been tried on a real file.

Music & Guitar

Deezer will let listeners switch AI-generated music off

A toggle hiding AI tracks, albums and related artists from search is due before the end of 2026; more than half of daily uploads are now AI.

A streaming service is a record store with an endless back room, and anyone can now fill that room with songs made from a prompt. Deezer is building a switch for listeners who would rather not browse those shelves.

The toggle hides AI-generated tracks, albums and related artists from search, and it is due before the end of 2026, TechCrunch reported on Oct 14. The scale explains why. In July, Deezer said more than half of its daily uploads were AI-generated, about 90,000 tracks a day, up from about 10% when it launched its AI detector in January 2025.

Listeners asked for it. A Deezer-Ipsos survey from November 2025 found roughly 45% of customers wanted a way to remove AI music. The switch puts that choice in the listener's hands instead of the platform deciding for everyone.

It lands as YouTube Music starts labeling songs as "Fully Gen AI", "Partly Gen AI" or "No Gen AI". One service labels, the other lets you filter; together they sketch where streaming is heading on AI music: disclosure first, and a choice for the listener.

The caveat: Deezer's toggle is a promise with a deadline, not a shipped feature yet.

Human Flourishing

Electric cars set sales-share records in 49 of 56 countries

Ember counts record battery-EV shares in 2026 and estimates oil importers saved about $20 billion in the six months after the Hormuz crisis began.

A sales share is the slice of all new cars sold that are fully battery-electric. Ember, which publishes primary energy data, tracks it in 56 countries, and in 2026, 49 of them hit a record battery-EV share.

The report's angle is energy security: how much imported oil those cars let a country skip. Every electric car on the road is a fuel tank that never has to be filled from a tanker. Ember estimates that oil importers saved about $20 billion in the six months after the Hormuz crisis began, with about 200 million barrels of crude displaced.

China carries most of it. From March to August, electric cars there displaced about $14 billion of crude, roughly three-quarters of the total for oil importers. The EU accounted for $3.1 billion.

This is a bad-news-makes-good-news receipt: a supply shock that made oil painful also made the cars that skip oil look better, and the buying records show people noticed.

The caveat: displaced oil is an estimate. Nobody can meter a barrel that was never burned, so these savings are calculated, not counted.

The Droplet Desk

  • NEW THIS WEEK — THE SCIENCE, PLAIN: [PUBLISHED] ATP is the molecule every living cell uses as a rechargeable battery, and making it normally takes enzymes, the protein machines that speed up reactions. A Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf team (William F. Martin and colleagues, "Hydrothermal origin of metabolic phosphorylation", FEBS Journal, open access, DOI 10.1111/febs.70737) showed that phosphite, a phosphorus compound found in hydrogen-producing hydrothermal vents, plus tiny catalytic amounts of palladium metal, can turn AMP into ADP with no enzyme at all: a half-empty battery charged one step. Think of the rock as the first charger, working before any cell existed. Palladium beat nickel, a natural palladium-iron-nickel alloy also worked, and the same chemistry added phosphate to ribose, glucose, glycerol and acetate. Martin calls it "a naturally formed geochemical precursor of ATP." This is a lab demonstration, not proof that life began this way.   • PLAIN PRIMER — [PUBLISHED] An RNA condensate is a cluster of RNA molecules that gathers into a liquid-like droplet with no membrane around it, a bit like oil beading up in vinaigrette. For origin-of-life research the appeal is that droplets could have held RNA strands close together and sheltered them in harsh early-Earth conditions, before cells had walls. Experiments can show that such droplets form and protect RNA under plausible conditions; they cannot show that this is how life actually began. The University at Buffalo's study (Nature Communications, July 31, 2026) found RNA forms droplets at about 10 C lower temperature than nearly identical DNA, because of a single oxygen-containing group on RNA's sugar.
